## Introduction

[Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }}) did not treat giants and fairies as relics of medieval imagination. In *The Book of the Damned*, he placed them alongside newspaper reports, archaeological curiosities, scientific notices and eyewitness accounts, deliberately refusing to separate folklore from apparently factual documentation. His purpose was not to prove that fairies or giants existed. Instead, he challenged the confidence with which modern institutions decided that some reports belonged to science while others belonged to legend. In doing so, Fort created one of the defining habits of [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}): examining stories about extraordinary beings not as settled truths or obvious fantasies, but as evidence that classification itself deserves scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or...Read more...</span></span></span>

This approach remains distinctive because it occupies an uncomfortable middle ground. [Folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}) explains why such beings appear in cultural tradition, while archaeology, anthropology and history test physical claims against material evidence. Fort deliberately blurred those boundaries, asking why reports of impossible beings continued to appear in modern printed sources long after educated society declared them extinct.

## Why did Fort file fairies beside facts?

Unlike a collector of myths, Fort searched [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}), scientific journals and antiquarian publications for reports that looked as though they had entered the documentary record before quietly disappearing from respectable discussion. Fairies and giants interested him because they illustrated how difficult it could be to draw a firm line between inherited folklore and modern testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or...Read more...</span></span></span>

His treatment differed from both believers and sceptics.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A believer might accept reports as literal encounters.
* A sceptic might dismiss them immediately as superstition or fraud.
* Fort instead asked why reports reached publication at all, why editors or scholars considered them worth recording, and why later generations felt obliged to explain them away.

For Fort, the existence of printed testimony was itself an anomaly. Even if every individual case proved mistaken, the persistence of such reports raised questions about how evidence entered—and later exited—the accepted record.

## Folklore categories in printed sources

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, folklore had become an organised field of study. Scholars collected oral traditions not because they believed every story, but because they regarded them as important cultural evidence. Newspapers likewise reported local discoveries, strange traditions and alleged archaeological finds with varying degrees of scepticism.

Fort exploited this mixed archive.

Instead of distinguishing sharply between folklore collections and scientific periodicals, he placed both into the same catalogue of anomalies. A newspaper account describing mysterious footprints, an antiquarian note about oversized burial remains, or a report of curious carved objects associated with "fairies" could all become part of his growing collection of excluded observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or...Read more...</span></span></span>

This was controversial because modern readers usually expect different standards of evidence from different publications. Fort intentionally resisted that hierarchy. His files suggested that credibility often depended less on the reported event than on where the report happened to be filed.

The result was not rigorous proof of extraordinary beings. Rather, it exposed how journalism, local history, folklore and scientific reporting often overlapped before later editors imposed cleaner disciplinary boundaries.

## Giants, fairies and credibility problems

Fort's discussions of giants and fairies illustrate recurring problems that continue to shape Fortean debates.

### Giant discoveries

Victorian and Edwardian newspapers frequently printed stories describing unusually large skeletons, oversized tools or mysterious prehistoric remains. Fort drew attention to such accounts because they appeared in seemingly respectable publications rather than purely fictional literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or...Read more...</span></span></span>

Modern archaeology provides little support for these reports. Many famous "giant skeleton" discoveries were never properly documented, involved misidentified animal remains, exaggerated measurements or outright newspaper [hoaxes]({{ 'hoaxes/' | relative_url }}). Despite repeated investigation, no verified archaeological evidence demonstrates the existence of a race of enormous humans beyond normal biological variation.

For Forteans, however, the important question is often historical rather than biological: why were these stories repeatedly published, circulated and then forgotten?

### Fairies in modern records

Fairies posed a different challenge.

Unlike giant skeletons, fairy reports rarely centred on physical remains. Instead they involved encounters, strange lights, miniature coffins, unusual stone objects or local traditions attached to specific landscapes. Fort noted these because they occupied an ambiguous space between folklore and observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or...Read more...</span></span></span>

Modern folklore research generally interprets these accounts as expressions of local belief, symbolic storytelling, social memory or psychological experience rather than evidence for hidden biological species. Yet Fort regarded their persistence as evidence that modern rationalism had not eliminated older ways of interpreting unusual events.

## How Forteana absorbed creature traditions

Fort helped transform creatures such as fairies and giants from purely folkloric figures into what later became "Fortean" subjects.

This shift involved several important changes.

**The setting became modern.** Reports increasingly came from newspapers, museum collections, archaeological claims and eyewitness testimony rather than medieval chronicles alone.

**The emphasis moved from belief to documentation.** Instead of asking whether fairies existed, Fortean writers asked why official records contained recurring references to impossible beings.

**Contradictions became valuable.** Cases that were incomplete, poorly explained or internally inconsistent interested Fort precisely because they resisted tidy classification.

This method influenced later anomalistic writing, where legends, [cryptids]({{ 'cryptids/' | relative_url }}), unexplained archaeology and eyewitness testimony are often discussed together—not because they share a common explanation, but because they challenge conventional boundaries between accepted knowledge and rejected reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

## What modern evidence actually shows

From the perspective of contemporary scholarship, the evidence for literal giants or fairies remains extremely weak.

Archaeology has developed far stricter excavation standards than those available in Fort's day. Claims involving giant skeletons now require documented excavation records, secure provenance, anatomical analysis and independent verification. Extraordinary claims almost never survive that process.

Similarly, historians of folklore have shown that fairy traditions differ dramatically across regions and periods, making it difficult to treat them as reports of a single objective phenomenon. Instead, they reflect changing religious ideas, local landscapes, social anxieties and storytelling traditions.

That does not eliminate the value of Fort's observations. Rather, it changes their significance. His archive demonstrates how difficult it can be to separate observation from interpretation once reports pass through newspapers, local memory, scientific institutions and popular culture. The strongest lesson is therefore methodological rather than supernatural: evidence is shaped by the systems that preserve, classify and reject it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

## Why this remains a Fortean question

Within Forteana, giants and fairies matter less as proposed species than as tests of intellectual boundaries. Fort deliberately assembled reports that appeared to belong simultaneously to folklore, journalism and scientific notice, forcing readers to confront an uncomfortable possibility: categories of knowledge are historically constructed rather than permanently fixed.

Modern research has explained many individual cases through folklore, [misidentification]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}), hoax, exaggeration or ordinary archaeology. Yet Fort's larger challenge survives. His files remind readers that the transition from reported event to accepted fact—or dismissed myth—is rarely automatic. It depends on institutions, standards of evidence and cultural expectations that evolve over time.
