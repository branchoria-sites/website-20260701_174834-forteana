## Introduction

The environmental DNA (eDNA) survey of [Loch Ness]({{ 'loch-ness/' | relative_url }}) changed the debate about the Loch Ness Monster by reframing it as a biological question rather than simply a legendary mystery. Instead of searching directly for a creature, researchers asked a more fundamental question: what animals actually leave detectable genetic traces in the loch? The results did not identify an unknown large vertebrate, but they did provide the most comprehensive genetic snapshot yet of the loch's ecosystem. That did not "disprove" every possible monster claim, but it substantially narrowed which explanations remain biologically plausible. Within the broader world of [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), the study illustrates how folklore can persist even as scientific evidence reshapes the range of realistic interpretations.

## The monster claim as biology

For decades, many proposed explanations for Nessie assumed the presence of a large, permanent animal population. Suggestions have ranged from surviving prehistoric reptiles and giant fish to oversized amphibians and unknown mammals. All of these ideas share one requirement: if such animals inhabit Loch Ness, they should leave biological evidence behind.

Environmental DNA offers a way to test that expectation. Every aquatic organism sheds tiny fragments of genetic material into its surroundings through skin cells, mucus, waste and decaying tissue. By collecting water samples and sequencing the DNA they contain, researchers can identify many of the species living in a body of water without ever capturing or directly observing them.

This approach does not search for a specific monster. Instead, it asks whether the ecosystem contains evidence consistent with the kinds of animals proposed in monster theories. In that sense, the Loch Ness project became less a monster hunt than a large-scale biodiversity survey using genetics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/first-edna-study-of-loch-ness-points-to-something-fishy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otago.ac.nz">[University of Otago]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otago.ac.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">first edna study of loch ness points to something fishy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of OtagoFirst eDNA Study Of Loch Ness Points To Something Fishy5 Sep 2019 — University of Otago geneticist, Professor Neil Gem...</span></span></span>

## What the Loch Ness DNA search tested

The project, led by Professor Neil Gemmell of the University of Otago, sampled approximately 250 water samples from locations around the loch, including surface water, deep water and shoreline sites. Researchers extracted DNA from each sample and compared millions of genetic sequences against international reference databases to identify the organisms present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/first-edna-study-of-loch-ness-points-to-something-fishy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otago.ac.nz">[University of Otago]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otago.ac.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">first edna study of loch ness points to something fishy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of OtagoFirst eDNA Study Of Loch Ness Points To Something Fishy5 Sep 2019 — University of Otago geneticist, Professor Neil Gem...</span></span></span>

The survey was designed to evaluate several broad classes of explanations for Nessie rather than to prove or disprove [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}) itself. Among the principal questions were whether the loch contained evidence for:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Large reptiles, such as the frequently proposed plesiosaur hypothesis.
* Large exotic fish, including catfish or sturgeon.
* Unexpected vertebrates that should be detectable through their DNA.
* Known native species that might account for eyewitness reports.

</div>

The findings were striking in what they did—and did not—show.

Researchers found abundant DNA from the organisms expected in a Highland freshwater ecosystem, including numerous fish, birds, mammals, amphibians and microorganisms. However, they found no evidence of reptilian DNA that would support the idea of a surviving prehistoric reptile. Likewise, they detected no genetic evidence for large resident animals such as catfish or sturgeon, both of which have occasionally been proposed as explanations for sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/first-edna-study-of-loch-ness-points-to-something-fishy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otago.ac.nz">[University of Otago+2BioTechniques]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otago.ac.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">first edna study of loch ness points to something fishy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of OtagoFirst eDNA Study Of Loch Ness Points To Something Fishy5 Sep 2019 — University of Otago geneticist, Professor Neil Gem...</span></span></span>

Importantly, the study did not claim that every individual animal in the loch would inevitably be detected. Environmental DNA depends on organisms shedding sufficient genetic material into sampled water, and extremely rare or transient visitors might escape detection. Nevertheless, the absence of DNA from any population of large unknown vertebrates substantially weakens hypotheses that require such animals to inhabit Loch Ness over long periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/first-edna-study-of-loch-ness-points-to-something-fishy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otago.ac.nz">[University of Otago]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otago.ac.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">first edna study of loch ness points to something fishy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of OtagoFirst eDNA Study Of Loch Ness Points To Something Fishy5 Sep 2019 — University of Otago geneticist, Professor Neil Gem...</span></span></span>

## Why eel explanations changed the debate

The most discussed outcome was not what was absent, but what was abundant.

European eel DNA appeared in large quantities throughout the samples. This did not demonstrate the existence of giant eels, because environmental DNA measures genetic presence rather than body size. A large amount of eel DNA could simply reflect many ordinary eels rather than a few exceptionally large ones. Professor Gemmell emphasised precisely this limitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.otago.ac.nz/news/newsroom/first-edna-study-of-loch-ness-points-to-something-fishy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: otago.ac.nz">[University of Otago]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">otago.ac.nz</span><span class="citation-popover-title">first edna study of loch ness points to something fishy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of OtagoFirst eDNA Study Of Loch Ness Points To Something Fishy5 Sep 2019 — University of Otago geneticist, Professor Neil Gem...</span></span></span>

Even so, the findings shifted the conversation because the eel hypothesis requires far fewer extraordinary assumptions than prehistoric survivors or unknown reptiles. European eels are already known to inhabit Loch Ness. If some individuals reached unusual sizes—or if ordinary eels were occasionally seen under poor viewing conditions—they could contribute to at least some reported sightings.

The study therefore altered the hierarchy of explanations:

* **Plesiosaur-style creatures** became considerably less plausible because no reptilian DNA was detected.
* **Large introduced fish**, such as catfish or sturgeon, also lost support through the absence of their genetic signatures.
* **Large eels** remained biologically conceivable, although unproven, because eels are unquestionably present in the loch and the DNA evidence is entirely consistent with their existence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2019/sep/05/loch-ness-monster-could-be-a-giant-eel-say-scientists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian+2lochnessproject.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianLoch Ness monster could be a giant eel, say scientists5 Sep 2019 — But Prof Neil Gemmell, who led the study, said he couldn&#x27;t...</span></span></span>

The key distinction is that the DNA evidence does not positively identify giant eels. Rather, it removes many alternative biological explanations while leaving one comparatively ordinary possibility still open.

## What environmental DNA can and cannot prove

The Loch Ness survey demonstrated the power of environmental DNA as an ecological tool, but it also highlighted its limits.

Environmental DNA excels at answering questions about the overall composition of an ecosystem. It can reveal which species are present, detect invasive organisms and monitor biodiversity without extensive trapping or visual surveys. These strengths have made it increasingly important in conservation biology and wildlife management well beyond Loch Ness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hudsonalpha.org/how-the-loch-ness-monster-could-help-save-marine-life/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hudsonalpha.org">[hudsonalpha.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hudsonalpha.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How the Loch Ness monster could help save marine life23 Sept 2019 — The researchers sequenced DNA from 250 samples of Loch Ness water, co...</span></span></span>

However, eDNA has important constraints:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It cannot reliably estimate the size of individual animals.
* It cannot determine whether DNA came from one large individual or many smaller ones.
* Rare, short-lived or recently arrived organisms may not be detected.
* Detection depends on available genetic reference databases and successful sampling.

</div>

Consequently, the absence of unknown monster DNA is powerful evidence against a long-term resident population of large undiscovered vertebrates, but it is not a logical proof that no unusual animal could ever pass through the loch.

## Why Nessie remains a Fortean icon

The environmental DNA survey transformed Nessie from a broad mystery into a more sharply defined scientific question. It demonstrated that cryptozoological claims can be evaluated using modern ecological methods rather than relying solely on eyewitness testimony or photography.

Yet the cultural life of the Loch Ness Monster survives largely untouched. The legend has always been more than a zoological hypothesis. It functions as local folklore, tourist attraction, media phenomenon and enduring symbol of the unknown. From a Fortean perspective, those roles continue regardless of whether environmental genetics narrows the biological possibilities.

The DNA study therefore represents a rare moment where folklore and modern molecular biology intersect. It did not end the Nessie story, but it changed the terms of the debate. Instead of asking whether any mysterious creature might inhabit Loch Ness, the evidence now asks a more precise question: given everything currently known about the loch's ecology, what kinds of animals could realistically be there?
