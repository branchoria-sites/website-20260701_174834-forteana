## Introduction

Many famous Fortean cases survive not because they were documented immediately, but because they were retold months, years or even decades later. That makes human memory one of the most important—and least appreciated—parts of the evidence. Modern psychological research shows that memory is not a recording stored unchanged in the mind. Instead, each act of remembering reconstructs an event, making it vulnerable to later conversations, interviews, newspaper accounts and personal reflection. This does not mean witnesses are dishonest. On the contrary, sincere people can confidently recall details that were added, altered or strengthened long after the original [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183265/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Neuroscience of Memory: Implications for the Courtroomby JW Lacy · 2013 · Cited by 268 — Research that specifically examines eyewi...</span></span></span>

For readers interested in [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), this matters because many celebrated anomalies depend almost entirely on recollections collected well after the event. Understanding how memory drifts helps explain why strange cases often grow more detailed over time without requiring fraud or deliberate embellishment. It also provides a practical framework for suspended judgment: treating witness testimony as valuable evidence while recognising that memory itself is part of the phenomenon that must be examined.

## Why memory changes after unusual events

An unusual experience is often emotionally powerful, surprising and difficult to interpret. Those qualities make it memorable, but they do not guarantee accurate recall. Research in cognitive [psychology]({{ 'psychology/' | relative_url }}) has consistently found that remembering is a reconstructive process. Each recollection draws on fragments of the original experience together with later knowledge, expectations and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10567586/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying false memoriesby AG Lentoor · 2023 · Cited by 13 — Memory distortions due to misinformation...</span></span></span>

For Fortean reports, several mechanisms are especially relevant:

* **Reconstruction rather than replay.** Memories are rebuilt whenever they are recalled, not replayed like a recording.
* **Source monitoring errors.** People may accurately remember a detail but misremember where it came from, confusing something they witnessed with something later read in a newspaper or heard from another witness.
* **The misinformation effect.** Later information can become incorporated into genuine memories, especially when presented by trusted or authoritative sources.
* **Schema-driven interpretation.** People naturally reshape memories to fit familiar cultural narratives or expectations, making odd events appear more coherent over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3213001/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2BPS PsychHub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCA Behavioral Account of the Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One cognitive account of the misinformation effect is that memory errors are due to source monitoring errors (Lindsay, 1990; Lindsay...R...</span></span></span>

These processes are ordinary features of memory rather than signs of pathology. They affect eyewitnesses in everyday settings and become even more significant when recollections concern confusing, unexpected or emotionally charged experiences.

One implication is particularly important for anomalous reports: confidence is not a reliable measure of accuracy. Studies of eyewitness memory repeatedly show only weak relationships between how certain people feel and whether their recollections are objectively correct. Confidence itself can increase after repeated questioning or positive feedback without improving accuracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183265/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Neuroscience of Memory: Implications for the Courtroomby JW Lacy · 2013 · Cited by 268 — Research that specifically examines eyewi...</span></span></span>

## How interviews and newspapers alter cases

Many classic paranormal narratives pass through several stages before reaching the public: an initial experience, conversations with family or friends, interviews by investigators, newspaper coverage, books and documentaries. Each stage introduces opportunities for memory to shift.

### Repeated interviews

Interviewing is necessary for documenting unusual experiences, but the style of questioning matters greatly. Suggestive questions can introduce details that witnesses later remember as part of the original event.

Experimental work by Elizabeth Loftus and colleagues demonstrated that even subtle wording changes can influence later recollection. Questions implying the existence of an object or emphasising dramatic action increase the likelihood that participants later report details they never actually observed. These findings have been replicated across decades of research into eyewitness testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lcrp.70020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS PsychHub+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BPS Psych Hub The history of an idea: The misinformation effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS PsychHubThe history of an idea: The misinformation effect - Loftus24 Dec 2025 — There it is defined as the notion that a person&#x27;s rec...</span></span></span>

For paranormal investigations, this means that questions such as "What colour was the glowing figure?" differ fundamentally from "Did you notice anything unusual?" The first assumes a figure existed; the second leaves the witness free to describe the experience without embedded suggestions.

### Newspaper influence

Media coverage can become part of a witness's later memory. Once an account is published, witnesses often read interpretations that include additional descriptions, speculative explanations or details supplied by others.

Psychologists describe this as post-event information. Rather than replacing the original memory entirely, later information may blend with authentic recollection until the witness no longer distinguishes between direct experience and subsequent reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lcrp.70020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS PsychHub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BPS Psych Hub The history of an idea: The misinformation effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS PsychHubThe history of an idea: The misinformation effect - Loftus24 Dec 2025 — There it is defined as the notion that a person&#x27;s rec...</span></span></span>

This process helps explain why older [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}) of famous Fortean cases sometimes become richer than the earliest surviving reports. New descriptive elements may enter gradually through repeated exposure rather than conscious invention.

### Conversations between witnesses

Witnesses rarely remain isolated after extraordinary events. They compare memories, discuss confusing details and attempt to construct a shared narrative.

Research on memory conformity shows that such discussions can cause witnesses' recollections to converge, even when some of the shared details were initially remembered by only one participant. The resulting account may feel more convincing because multiple people agree, while actually reflecting mutual influence rather than independent confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misinformation_effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Misinformation effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation effect</span></span></span>

## Why strange experiences are especially vulnerable

Paranormal reports possess characteristics that make memory distortion particularly plausible without implying that the original experience was imaginary.

First, anomalous events often unfold rapidly under poor viewing conditions or during moments of surprise. Witnesses may immediately begin interpreting what they saw because they lack an obvious explanation.

Second, uncertainty encourages later reconstruction. When people remember incomplete experiences, they naturally fill gaps using prior knowledge and expectations. Someone familiar with UFO stories may interpret an ambiguous light differently from someone whose background is aviation or astronomy.

Third, extraordinary experiences are often retold repeatedly. Every retelling provides another opportunity for reconstruction. Details may become more coherent, emotionally satisfying or culturally recognisable while drifting away from the earliest recollection.

This gradual evolution differs from deliberate hoaxing. A witness can sincerely believe an increasingly elaborate narrative because each reconstruction feels subjectively authentic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10567586/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying false memoriesby AG Lentoor · 2023 · Cited by 13 — Memory distortions due to misinformation...</span></span></span>

## What fresh records can preserve

Because memory changes over time, investigators generally place greater evidential weight on records created close to the original event.

Useful contemporary records include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* contemporaneous notebooks or diaries;
* timestamped photographs or recordings;
* emergency calls or official reports;
* independent witness statements collected separately;
* newspaper reports published before extensive public discussion;
* physical observations documented before interpretation becomes established.

</div>

[Early records]({{ 'early-records/' | relative_url }}) are not automatically accurate, but they are less exposed to later contamination than recollections gathered years afterwards. Comparing successive versions of the same account can also reveal how narratives evolved, highlighting which elements remained stable and which appeared only after repeated retellings.

For Fortean research, preserving the earliest available wording is often more valuable than producing the most polished narrative. Small omissions, uncertainty and ambiguity may represent authentic features of the original experience rather than weaknesses to be corrected.

## What memory drift means for suspended judgment

Recognising false-memory risks neither proves nor disproves paranormal claims. Instead, it changes how testimony should be evaluated.

A witness may honestly report an unusual event while later remembering additional features that entered through interviews, discussion or media exposure. The core experience and later elaborations should therefore be assessed separately whenever possible.

Within the Fortean tradition of suspended judgment, this distinction encourages proportionate confidence. Stable details documented close to the event deserve more evidential weight than dramatic additions appearing only after years of retelling. Likewise, inconsistencies need not imply deception; they may reflect the normal workings of reconstructive memory.

Viewed this way, memory drift is not merely a sceptical objection. It is itself part of the evidence. Understanding how recollections evolve allows investigators to separate the original anomaly from the later story built around it, preserving both curiosity about unexplained experiences and caution about the limitations of human remembrance.
