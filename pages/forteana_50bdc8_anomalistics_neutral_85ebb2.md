## Introduction

Anomalistics is the disciplined study of claims that appear to fall outside [ordinary explanation]({{ 'ordinary-causes/' | relative_url }}), without deciding at the start whether they are genuine discoveries, mistakes, hoaxes, folklore, psychological effects or misclassified natural events. Within [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), it matters because it turns “strange stories” into examinable claims: What exactly was reported? What evidence exists? Which explanations have been ruled out? What would count as a better test? The field’s value is not that it proves ghosts, cryptids, UFOs or other extraordinary phenomena. Its value is procedural: it offers a neutral way to handle anomalous reports before belief, ridicule or institutional embarrassment harden into a conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/anomalistics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaAnomalistics - Psi Encyclopedia13 Apr 2015 — Anomalistics takes unexplained claims seriously without committing itself to...</span></span></span>

That makes anomalistics a governance practice as much as an intellectual curiosity. It asks how researchers, public agencies, journalists and archives should treat claims that are uncertain but socially persistent. A neutral claim study does not give every claim equal credibility; it gives every claim a fair pathway through definition, documentation, alternative explanations and [evidence standards]({{ 'evidence-standard/' | relative_url }}). This is especially important in Forteana, where reports often cross boundaries between science, folklore, [psychology]({{ 'psychology/' | relative_url }}), security, media and personal experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newdualism.org/papers/M.Truzzi/TruzzionScientificUnorthodoxy.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newdualism.org">[newdualism.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newdualism.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truzzi on Scientific UnorthodoxyMy general concern is to try to foster an interdisciplinary program, best called anomalistics, on the stu...</span></span></span>

## How anomalistics differs from Forteana

Forteana is the wider cultural field: a tradition of collecting and discussing odd reports that seem to resist accepted categories. Anomalistics is narrower and more method-conscious. It formalises the question, “How should we study this anomaly?” rather than simply preserving the anomaly as a curiosity. The Society for Psychical Research’s Psi Encyclopedia describes anomalistics as taking unexplained claims seriously without committing to one outcome, and as treating anomalies as opportunities for disciplined inquiry rather than immediate belief or [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/anomalistics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaAnomalistics - Psi Encyclopedia13 Apr 2015 — Anomalistics takes unexplained claims seriously without committing itself to...</span></span></span>

The distinction is easiest to see in the move from collection to evaluation. [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }}) gathered “damned” facts to challenge the complacency of official explanation. Later anomalistics kept the Fortean interest in rejected or awkward data, but added a stronger demand for method: classification, source criticism, cross-disciplinary expertise, replication where possible, and careful separation between the report itself and the interpretation placed upon it. Roger W. Wescott is widely credited with coining “anomalistics” in 1973, defining it as the serious and systematic study of phenomena that do not fit common sense or established scientific pictures of reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalistics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

This difference prevents two common errors. The first is credulity: treating an unexplained report as if it automatically supports the most dramatic hypothesis. The second is premature debunking: treating an odd claim as worthless because it sounds implausible. Anomalistics instead keeps the claim open long enough to ask what kind of claim it is. A light in the sky may be an aviation issue, an astronomical misidentification, a sensor artefact, a military secrecy problem, a witness-memory problem, a hoax, or a genuinely unresolved observation. Neutral inquiry begins by refusing to collapse all those possibilities into one story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newdualism.org/papers/M.Truzzi/TruzzionScientificUnorthodoxy.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newdualism.org">[newdualism.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newdualism.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truzzi on Scientific UnorthodoxyMy general concern is to try to foster an interdisciplinary program, best called anomalistics, on the stu...</span></span></span>

## Neutral inquiry into claims

Neutral claim study is not neutrality about truth. It is neutrality about procedure. The investigator does not promise that the extraordinary claim is likely, but also does not decide in advance that the witness, claimant or archive must be wrong. In practical terms, this means distinguishing four things that are often blurred together: the experience someone reports, the record of that report, the proposed explanation, and the wider belief system that grows around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/84781893/Introduction_Single_case_studies_in_anomalistics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

Marcello Truzzi, a sociologist closely associated with organised scepticism and anomalistics, argued for an interdisciplinary study of facts that seem unexplained by current models. His point was that an anomaly’s final home is not known at the start. A UFO case, for example, may end up belonging to astronomy, sociology, psychology, meteorology or military technology. That uncertainty is why anomalistics requires procedural humility: the first task is not to pick a side, but to build a case file that different kinds of expertise can examine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newdualism.org/papers/M.Truzzi/TruzzionScientificUnorthodoxy.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newdualism.org">[newdualism.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newdualism.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truzzi on Scientific UnorthodoxyMy general concern is to try to foster an interdisciplinary program, best called anomalistics, on the stu...</span></span></span>

A neutral study normally asks several linked questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **What is the minimum claim?** “A witness saw a moving light at 21:10” is different from “an alien craft flew overhead”.
* **What is the evidence chain?** First-hand testimony, photographs, instrument logs, medical records, newspaper retellings and later folklore do not carry the same weight.
* **What ordinary explanations fit?** Misperception, coincidence, fraud, equipment error, environmental conditions and cultural expectation must be tested before exotic explanations.
* **What remains unexplained after testing?** A residue of uncertainty is not the same as proof of the paranormal.
* **What new observation would change the assessment?** A good anomalistic study defines what better evidence would look like.

</div>

This approach is useful because many anomalous claims are socially real even when their proposed explanations are not. People do report exceptional experiences; communities do preserve legends; governments do receive UAP reports; scientists do encounter outliers in data. Anomalistics asks how to handle such material without either inflating it into revelation or discarding it as embarrassment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/paranormal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apa.org.</span></span></span>

## Where scientific evaluation helps most

Scientific evaluation helps anomalistics most when it improves the quality of the question. The strongest contribution is often not a spectacular answer, but better discrimination between cases that can be explained, cases that are too poorly documented to assess, and cases that deserve fresh data collection. NASA’s 2023 independent study on unidentified anomalous phenomena made this point directly: without extensive, well-characterised data, many observations are nearly impossible to verify or explain, so the immediate need is better data acquisition rather than dramatic interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nasa.gov.</span></span></span>

UAP research shows why this matters. Many historical cases are built from witness testimony, incomplete sensor records, military secrecy and retrospective interpretation. The US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office reported in 2024 that it had found no verifiable evidence that any UAP sighting represented extraterrestrial activity, and no evidence that the US government or private industry had access to extraterrestrial technology. That finding does not mean every sighting was neatly explained; it means the strongest official review did not support the most extraordinary popular claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.war.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/3701297/dod-report-discounts-sightings-of-extraterrestrial-technology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: war.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">war.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dod report discounts sightings of extraterrestrial technology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod report discounts sightings of extraterrestrial technology</span></span></span>

At the same time, scientific evaluation can make anomaly study more serious by moving it away from anecdote. The Galileo Project, for instance, has argued for instrumented, transparent and systematic [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) of aerial phenomena, including wide-field cameras, narrow-field characterisation, passive radar methods, radio monitoring, microphones and environmental sensors. The important shift is governance-oriented: replace accidental sightings and rumour chains with calibrated, time-stamped, multi-sensor records that can be checked by others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

This standard also protects against a subtle mistake: treating “unidentified” as a positive category. In anomalistics, unidentified means the present evidence does not justify a settled identification. It does not by itself establish aliens, spirits, unknown animals or new physics. Better instruments, metadata, replication and independent review matter because they narrow the space of uncertainty. In science generally, reproducibility and replicability are central ways of testing whether a finding is dependable, though the National Academies notes that failure to replicate can itself sometimes help scientific discovery by exposing hidden assumptions or boundary conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547537/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIReproducibility and Replicability in Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBIReproducibility and Replicability in Science</span></span></span>

## The psychological layer is not a dismissal

Anomalistics often overlaps with anomalistic psychology, which studies unusual experiences and paranormal beliefs through known or testable psychological processes. This is sometimes misunderstood as a polite way of saying “the witness imagined it”. That is too crude. Psychological evaluation can explain how sincere people can have vivid, meaningful and mistaken experiences, but it can also clarify why some claims become culturally powerful and why memory, expectation and interpretation matter in evidence gathering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/paranormal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apa.org.</span></span></span>

Chris French and other anomalistic psychologists have argued that paranormal claims are useful for studying perception, memory, interpretation and belief. Reports of ghosts, alien encounters, psychic impressions or uncanny coincidences often involve ordinary cognitive processes operating under unusual emotional, environmental or social conditions. This does not require accusing witnesses of dishonesty. It means recognising that human perception is not a recording device, and that reports should be handled with both empathy and evidential care.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sydneysymposium.unsw.edu.au/2024/chapters/FrenchSSSP2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sydneysymposium.unsw.edu.au">[Sydney Symposium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sydneysymposium.unsw.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sydney Symposium The Psychology of Paranormal Beliefs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sydney Symposium The Psychology of Paranormal Beliefs</span></span></span>

This matters for Forteana because witness experience is often the starting point. A neutral claim study can respect the person while testing the claim. It can say: the experience was real to the witness; the interpretation may be wrong; the record may be incomplete; and the case may still be worth studying because it reveals something about perception, environment, institutions or culture. That layered response is more useful than the familiar argument between “believers” and “debunkers”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dokumen.pub/anomalistic-psychology-exploring-paranormal-belief-and-experience-9781403995711-9781137368065.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dokumen.pub">[dokumen.pub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dokumen.pub</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dokumen.pub.</span></span></span>

## Evidence standards without automatic debunking

The famous principle that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence is closely associated with Carl Sagan, but Marcello Truzzi used a similar formulation before it became widely popularised. The principle is often useful: claims that would overturn well-supported knowledge should not be accepted on thin testimony or ambiguous data. Yet anomalistics also asks how such standards are applied. If “extraordinary” becomes a rhetorical label rather than an evidential threshold, it can turn into a way of dismissing uncomfortable claims before they are examined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraordinary_claims_require_extraordinary_evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence</span></span></span>

A practical anomalistic standard is proportional rather than theatrical. A blurry photograph may be enough to justify asking what camera, time, location and weather conditions were involved. It is not enough to establish a new species, a haunting or alien technology. A repeated pattern of instrumented observations may justify deeper investigation. A single second-hand story may be culturally interesting but scientifically weak. The evidence required depends on the consequence of the proposed explanation, the availability of ordinary alternatives, and the quality of the record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs8 May 2026 —... UAP. In 2023, NASA commissioned the UAP Independent Study Team to examine unidentified anomalous phe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2026</span></span></span>

This is where governance matters. Institutions need procedures for triage, not just opinions. A serious anomalistic process can separate reports into categories: explained, probably explained, insufficient data, unresolved but low evidential value, and unresolved with enough quality to merit further study. That prevents two forms of institutional failure: wasting resources on every sensational claim, and ignoring weak signals because they arrive in culturally disreputable packaging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nasa.gov.</span></span></span>

## Why institutions need a neutral pathway

Anomalous claims create institutional discomfort because they sit between reputational risk and public interest. Agencies, universities, museums, archives and media organisations may fear that studying them gives them legitimacy. But refusing to study them can also feed suspicion, conspiracy thinking and poor public understanding. A neutral pathway gives institutions a way to say: “This claim is not endorsed, but it can be documented, checked and classified.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/mar/08/pentagon-ufo-report-hiding-aliens" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The Society for Anomalistics offers one model of this posture. It describes itself as supporting a critical but open-minded approach to controversial scientific claims and exceptional human experiences, while its associated journal presents itself as a peer-reviewed forum for empirical, methodological, conceptual, philosophical and historical work on anomalies and para-sciences. This does not make every claim credible. It creates a venue where contested claims can be examined under explicit standards rather than left entirely to sensational media or closed belief communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.anomalistik.de/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anomalistik.de">[anomalistik.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anomalistik.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on anomalistik.de.</span></span></span>

For public bodies, the policy lesson is not “believe the anomaly”. It is “design a process fit for uncertainty”. That means clear intake forms, preservation of original records, metadata standards, conflict-of-interest rules, expert review, privacy protection for witnesses, and public communication that distinguishes unresolved cases from extraordinary conclusions. NASA’s UAP work and AARO’s historical review show two sides of the same need: better data collection for future cases, and careful review of historical claims whose evidence may be fragmented, classified, culturally amplified or technically weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nasa.gov.</span></span></span>

## What anomalistics contributes to Forteana

Anomalistics gives Forteana a disciplined middle path. It preserves Fort’s suspicion that official knowledge can sometimes ignore awkward facts, but it rejects the temptation to treat every awkward fact as a suppressed truth. It keeps curiosity alive while asking for better records, better categories and better tests. That is why it is one of the most useful bridges between Fortean culture and scientific evaluation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/anomalistics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaAnomalistics - Psi Encyclopedia13 Apr 2015 — Anomalistics takes unexplained claims seriously without committing itself to...</span></span></span>

Its most important contribution is not a catalogue of conclusions, but a habit of inquiry. A strange report becomes more valuable when the claim is narrowed, the evidence chain is mapped, ordinary explanations are tested, uncertainty is named honestly, and future evidence requirements are specified. That method can apply to UFOs, anomalous animals, poltergeist claims, unusual falls from the sky, exceptional experiences and disputed historical cases without forcing them into a single worldview.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newdualism.org/papers/M.Truzzi/TruzzionScientificUnorthodoxy.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newdualism.org">[newdualism.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newdualism.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truzzi on Scientific UnorthodoxyMy general concern is to try to foster an interdisciplinary program, best called anomalistics, on the stu...</span></span></span>

In that sense, anomalistics is Forteana’s best safeguard against both credulity and contempt. It allows a public culture of strange claims to remain interesting without becoming careless. It also allows science-facing institutions to investigate uncertainty without pretending that uncertainty is proof. The result is a more mature way to handle the anomalous: open enough to notice what does not fit, strict enough to ask what the evidence can really bear.
