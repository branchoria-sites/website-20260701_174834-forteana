## Introduction

Were UFO questions already present in [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})'s files before the term "flying saucer" existed? In an important sense, yes—but not because Fort documented modern UFOs. Rather, *The Book of the Damned* assembled a long trail of reports describing unexplained lights, luminous objects, strange aerial forms and unusual atmospheric events from well before 1947. These accounts became a template for later Fortean and UFO researchers, who argued that unexplained aerial phenomena had a much deeper history than the post-war saucer era. At the same time, many of the reports remain difficult to verify, and historians caution that newspaper sensationalism, [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}) and incomplete records complicate attempts to interpret them as evidence of a single continuing phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.encyclopedia.com/science-and-technology/technology/aviation-general/ufo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.com">[Encyclopedia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In fact, the first UFO book, Charles Fort &#x27;s The Book of the Damned, was published in 1919.... books by an equally influential theorist...</span></span></span>

## Strange lights before 1947

Fort's contribution was not to discover unknown events but to collect scattered printed reports that had largely been forgotten. Working through [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}), scientific journals and magazines, he assembled examples that seemed to resist straightforward explanation. Within his broader catalogue of "damned" data were numerous descriptions of unusual appearances in the sky.

Several recurring patterns appear in the material that later readers associated with UFO history:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Persistent luminous objects** reported by multiple witnesses.
* **Objects described as discs, cigars, globes or elongated lights**, although the terminology reflected the technology and language of their own era rather than later "flying saucer" imagery.
* **Unexpected aerial manoeuvres** that observers considered inconsistent with known aircraft or astronomical bodies.
* **Repeated newspaper reports** from different locations describing comparable aerial curiosities.

</div>

Fort rarely presented these as proof of extraterrestrial visitation. Instead, he argued that the existence of numerous awkward reports deserved attention because they had been dismissed too quickly or absorbed into conventional explanations without adequate examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_Damned" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Book of the Damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Book of the Damned</span></span></span>

One of Fort's recurring themes involved mysterious atmospheric lights that appeared before accepted scientific explanations existed. He pointed, for example, to unusual glowing skies reported before the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa, questioning whether later explanations had been applied too broadly to earlier observations. His purpose was less to prove a mystery than to illustrate how scientific consensus sometimes develops after the fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_Damned" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Book of the Damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Book of the Damned</span></span></span>

Another important category consisted of nineteenth-century reports of unexplained aerial craft. These accounts usually described elongated, dirigible-like objects rather than metallic discs. Because powered flight itself was a new technological dream, witnesses interpreted unknown aerial objects through the vocabulary available to them.

The best-known examples are the American "mystery airship" waves of 1896–97. Newspapers carried hundreds of reports of illuminated airships, strange lights and occasional claims of encounters with their occupants. Modern historians generally regard these reports as a mixture of misidentifications, rumours, [hoaxes]({{ 'hoaxes/' | relative_url }}) and genuine unexplained observations, but they remain important because they demonstrate that widespread aerial mystery narratives existed half a century before the Kenneth Arnold sighting of 1947.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_airship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mystery airship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mystery airship</span></span></span>

## Why later UFO writers mined Fort

After 1947, researchers searching for historical precedents naturally returned to Fort's compilations. His books had already assembled thousands of reports that seemed to resemble aspects of the emerging UFO phenomenon.

The influence worked in several ways.

First, Fort provided **an archive rather than a theory**. Later investigators did not need to begin historical research from scratch because he had already identified newspapers and journals containing unusual aerial reports.

Second, he encouraged **comparative reading across centuries**. Instead of treating each case as isolated, Fort juxtaposed reports from different countries and decades, inviting readers to look for recurring patterns without insisting they all had a common cause.

Third, he established the Fortean habit of distinguishing **the report from the explanation**. A witness might sincerely report an extraordinary light without that automatically confirming any particular interpretation. This distinction later became influential among researchers who preferred to discuss unidentified aerial phenomena without immediately concluding they represented extraterrestrial spacecraft.

Researchers such as Jacques Vallée expanded this historical approach, arguing that reports of unexplained aerial phenomena long predated modern UFO culture. Vallée's historical catalogues drew upon many of the same nineteenth-century and earlier sources that Fort had helped popularise, although Vallée generally attempted more systematic source criticism than Fort himself employed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/JacquesValleeChrisAubeckWondersInTheSkyUnexplainedAerialObjectsFromAntiquityToModernTimes/Jacques%2BVallee%2C%2BChris%2BAubeck%2BWonders%2Bin%2Bthe%2BSky%2B%2BUnexplained%2BAerial%2BObjects%2Bfrom%2BAntiquity%2Bto%2BModern%2BTimes_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive+2Encyclopedia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Wonders In The Sky</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveFull text of &quot;Wonders In The Sky - Unexplained Aerial...Wonders in the sky: unexplained aerial objects from antiquity to...</span></span></span>

## What source-checking can and cannot prove

The greatest strength of Fort's aerial catalogue is also its greatest weakness.

Most of his material originated in published newspapers and journals rather than private anecdotes. That means many reports can, at least in principle, be traced back to identifiable publications. Fort's cases therefore remain valuable starting points for historical investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fort" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Charles Fort</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charles Fort</span></span></span>

However, several important limitations remain.

**Many original reports survive only in abbreviated newspaper form.** Journalists often rewrote local stories, omitted witness details or repeated material from other papers, making it difficult to determine how many genuinely independent observations existed.

**Sensational journalism influenced reporting.** The late nineteenth century was an era of intense newspaper competition, and dramatic aerial stories could spread rapidly between publications, accumulating embellishments as they travelled. The mystery [airship wave]({{ 'airship-wave/' | relative_url }}) is a classic example of this process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_airship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mystery airship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mystery airship</span></span></span>

**Modern terminology cannot simply be projected backwards.** Witnesses described what they believed they saw using the concepts available in their own time. An observer in 1897 might compare an unknown object to an airship; someone in 1947 might call a similar sighting a flying saucer; a modern observer may describe a drone or unidentified aerial phenomenon. Similar descriptions do not necessarily indicate the same underlying cause.

**Some reports have acquired legendary status through repetition.** Cases copied from one anomaly collection to another can gradually appear stronger than their documentary foundations justify. Modern historians therefore attempt to return to the earliest available newspaper or journal rather than relying on later [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}).

For these reasons, Fort's files function better as **historical evidence trails** than as established evidence for any specific explanation. They point researchers towards recurring reports that deserve examination but rarely settle the question themselves.

## Why these early sky reports still matter in Forteana

Within the Fortean tradition, the significance of pre-1947 sky anomalies lies less in demonstrating that extraterrestrial craft visited earlier centuries than in challenging the assumption that aerial mysteries began with the flying saucer era.

Fort showed that unexplained lights, strange aerial objects and disputed atmospheric phenomena had already entered printed public discourse decades before modern UFO terminology existed. Whether later readers interpret those reports as misidentifications, cultural myths, atmospheric phenomena, secret technology or genuinely unexplained events, the historical record demonstrates continuity in the questions people asked about unusual things seen in the sky.
