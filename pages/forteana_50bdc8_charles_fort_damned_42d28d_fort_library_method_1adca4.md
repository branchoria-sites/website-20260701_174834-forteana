## Introduction

[Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})'s anomaly archive was remarkable not because he travelled the world investigating mysteries, but because he turned libraries into laboratories. Rather than interviewing witnesses or collecting physical evidence, Fort spent years reading scientific journals, [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}) and magazines in search of reports that appeared to contradict accepted knowledge. This method gave him extraordinary breadth. He could compare obscure reports separated by decades, countries and scientific disciplines, identifying recurring patterns that specialists rarely considered together. At the same time, it imposed severe limits. Most of his evidence was second-hand, filtered through editors, journalists and correspondents whose accuracy he could seldom verify. Understanding this tension is essential to understanding both the enduring appeal and the principal weakness of Fort's work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kar.kent.ac.uk/50907/1/C.%20Sleigh%20-%20An%20outcry%20of%20silence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kar.kent.ac.uk">[Kent Academic Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kar.kent.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kent Academic RepositoryCharles Hoy Fort and the uncanny voices of scienceSeptember 27, 2016 — by C Sleigh · 2016 · Cited by 4 — Fort was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 27, 2016</span></span></span>

## Libraries as anomaly mines

Fort's research was built around reading rather than [fieldwork]({{ 'fieldwork/' | relative_url }}). After abandoning earlier unpublished manuscripts known as *X* and *Y*, he devoted years to searching printed sources in the reading rooms of the British Museum (now the British Library collections) and the New York Public Library. These institutions gave him access to scientific periodicals, learned society publications, local newspapers and popular magazines spanning long periods and multiple countries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kar.kent.ac.uk/50907/1/C.%20Sleigh%20-%20An%20outcry%20of%20silence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kar.kent.ac.uk">[Kent Academic Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kar.kent.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kent Academic RepositoryCharles Hoy Fort and the uncanny voices of scienceSeptember 27, 2016 — by C Sleigh · 2016 · Cited by 4 — Fort was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 27, 2016</span></span></span>

His approach differed sharply from that of an investigative journalist or naturalist. Instead of asking whether an individual report could be confirmed, Fort searched for accumulation. One unexplained rain of frogs might be dismissed as an error. Fifty similar reports scattered through different publications became, in his view, evidence that conventional explanations deserved re-examination.

This archival strategy offered several advantages.

* It exposed forgotten observations buried in specialist literature.
* It allowed comparisons across astronomy, meteorology, geology, zoology and [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}).
* It highlighted anomalies that had received little follow-up despite appearing in otherwise respectable publications.
* It reduced dependence on any single institution or scientific discipline.

Fort therefore treated libraries not simply as repositories of accepted knowledge but as vast collections of overlooked observations waiting to be rearranged into new patterns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kar.kent.ac.uk/50907/1/C.%20Sleigh%20-%20An%20outcry%20of%20silence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kar.kent.ac.uk">[Kent Academic Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kar.kent.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kent Academic RepositoryCharles Hoy Fort and the uncanny voices of scienceSeptember 27, 2016 — by C Sleigh · 2016 · Cited by 4 — Fort was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 27, 2016</span></span></span>

## Reach across time and disciplines

One of the strengths of Fort's library method was its ability to connect reports that had originally appeared in completely different contexts.

A provincial newspaper might print an account of unusual material falling from the sky. A scientific society could publish a short note describing an unexplained atmospheric event. A natural history journal might record an animal behaving in an unexpected way. Individually, such reports often disappeared into archives. Fort deliberately assembled them into thematic collections.

This method anticipated, in a loose sense, later forms of comparative database research. Rather than treating each anomaly as an isolated curiosity, he looked for repetition across geography and history. Repeated reports of mysterious aerial objects, unusual falls of organic material or unexplained [disappearances]({{ 'disappearances/' | relative_url }}) interested him because they suggested recurring phenomena rather than isolated [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}).

Importantly, Fort rarely claimed that any single report proved an extraordinary conclusion. Instead, he argued that the cumulative existence of many difficult reports challenged excessive confidence in complete scientific explanations. His books therefore function more as catalogues of unresolved questions than as collections of demonstrated facts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — Rains of blood and frogs, mysterious disappea...</span></span></span>

## The problem of second-hand reports

The same features that made Fort's archive unusually broad also made it difficult to evaluate.

Almost every stage of the evidence chain involved another person's judgement.

A typical anomaly might pass through several layers before reaching Fort:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. An observer witnessed an unusual event.
2. A local correspondent described it.
3. A newspaper editor shortened or rewrote the account.
4. Another publication reprinted it.
5. Fort extracted the report years later.

</div>

At each stage, details could be lost, exaggerated or misunderstood. By the time Fort encountered the account, opportunities for interviewing witnesses, examining locations or collecting physical evidence had usually disappeared.

This dependence on printed testimony meant that Fort often could not distinguish between accurate [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}), reporting error, misunderstanding, folklore, satire or deliberate fabrication. Modern historians of science therefore view his archive as an important record of anomalous reporting rather than a verified catalogue of anomalous events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kar.kent.ac.uk/50907/1/C.%20Sleigh%20-%20An%20outcry%20of%20silence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kar.kent.ac.uk">[Kent Academic Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kar.kent.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kent Academic RepositoryCharles Hoy Fort and the uncanny voices of scienceSeptember 27, 2016 — by C Sleigh · 2016 · Cited by 4 — Fort was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 27, 2016</span></span></span>

## Why newspaper clippings are not the same as evidence

Fort frequently cited reports originating in newspapers because newspapers documented unusual local events that scientific journals ignored. However, newspapers have long mixed careful reporting with hearsay, sensationalism and editorial embellishment.

Several problems recur throughout Fort's sources:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Variable witness quality.** Many reports identify no eyewitnesses or provide only brief summaries.
* **Missing follow-up.** Initial reports often lack later investigation that might confirm or explain the event.
* **Editorial compression.** Newspapers routinely shortened complex incidents, removing context that later readers would need.
* **Duplicate circulation.** One original story could be reprinted by numerous newspapers, creating the appearance of independent corroboration when there was only a single source.
* **Changing standards.** Nineteenth-century reporting practices varied enormously between publications and regions.

</div>

These limitations do not automatically make the reports false. They simply mean that clipping an account from print is very different from independently verifying what occurred.

This distinction is one of the central criticisms made by both sceptical commentators and sympathetic historians. Fort successfully demonstrated that unusual reports existed in abundance; he did not establish that each report accurately described reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/newsletter/charles-fort-purveyor-of-the-unprobed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical InquirerCharles Fort: Purveyor of the Unprobedby J Nickell — He spent the remainder of his life scouring old newspapers and oth...</span></span></span>

## Arrangement as argument

Fort did more than collect reports. He carefully arranged them.

His books move rapidly from one anomaly to another, often presenting dozens of examples before offering commentary. This editorial technique creates an impression of overwhelming accumulation. Readers encounter repeated examples before having time to investigate any single case in depth.

Critics have argued that this style can magnify weak evidence by sheer volume. If dozens of poorly documented reports are presented consecutively, the collection may appear stronger than the individual cases warrant.

Supporters respond that this was precisely Fort's point. He wanted readers to question the assumption that every inconvenient observation had already been adequately explained. His books were intended as provocations against intellectual certainty rather than conventional scientific monographs. ## How modern readers should assess Fort's archive

Fort's archive is most valuable when understood as a historical collection of anomalous claims rather than a repository of established discoveries.

Modern researchers typically separate three questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Did the report actually appear in print?** In many cases, yes; Fort often quoted genuine publications.
* **Did the reported event really occur as described?** Frequently impossible to determine from surviving evidence alone.
* **Does the report demonstrate a failure of accepted science?** Usually not by itself, although it may identify an observation deserving closer investigation.

</div>

Seen this way, Fort's greatest contribution was methodological rather than evidential. He demonstrated how institutional knowledge is shaped by selection as well as discovery. Libraries contained not only accepted facts but also abandoned observations, forgotten puzzles and unresolved claims. Whether those claims represented genuine anomalies, reporting errors or ordinary events distorted by transmission remained a separate question requiring investigation beyond the clipping itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kar.kent.ac.uk/50907/1/C.%20Sleigh%20-%20An%20outcry%20of%20silence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kar.kent.ac.uk">[Kent Academic Repository+2Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kar.kent.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kent Academic RepositoryCharles Hoy Fort and the uncanny voices of scienceSeptember 27, 2016 — by C Sleigh · 2016 · Cited by 4 — Fort was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 27, 2016</span></span></span>
