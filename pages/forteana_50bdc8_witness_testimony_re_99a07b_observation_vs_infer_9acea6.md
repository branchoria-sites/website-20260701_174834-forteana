## Introduction

The most useful witness testimony in Fortean investigations often begins by removing the explanation and preserving the [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}). A report that says, “I saw a glowing sphere hovering above the trees for twenty seconds” contains an observation. A report that says, “I saw an alien craft” contains an interpretation. The difference matters because observations can be tested against [weather]({{ 'weather/' | relative_url }}), astronomy, aircraft movements, geography and other witness accounts, whereas interpretations reflect the witness's attempt to explain what those observations meant.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_witness_testimony_re_99a07b_observation_vs_infer_9acea6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Observation illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_witness_testimony_re_99a07b_observation_vs_infer_9acea6-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_witness_testimony_re_99a07b_observation_vs_infer_9acea6-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
This distinction does not imply that witnesses are dishonest or unreliable. On the contrary, sincere witnesses almost always combine direct perception with immediate interpretation because the human brain naturally tries to make unfamiliar events meaningful. The challenge for investigators is therefore not to reject testimony but to separate what was actually perceived from the conclusions attached to it. That process preserves the evidence that remains useful even if later explanations change.

## What Did the Witness Actually Report?

A practical way to assess any strange-event account is to divide every statement into two categories:

ObservationInterpretation"There was a white light above the hill.""It was a UFO.""The object changed direction suddenly.""It was intelligently controlled.""I heard three loud knocks from inside the wall.""Something paranormal was trying to communicate.""A dark figure crossed the road.""It was a cryptid."

Only the first column consists of sensory observations. The second consists of inferences.

This distinction is standard practice in investigative interviewing because memory is reconstructive rather than photographic. Witnesses do not merely replay stored recordings; they reconstruct events from perception, memory and later knowledge. As psychologists studying eyewitness testimony have repeatedly shown, later questioning, discussion with others and repeated recollection can gradually blend observation with interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/62246/chapter/550960878?searchresult=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Academic Eyewitness Memory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicEyewitness Memory - Oxford Academic22 Mar 2023 — Indeed, the very act of testing memory changes (i.e., contaminates) memory...</span></span></span>

In Fortean cases this matters especially because unusual experiences often lack familiar reference points. Faced with something unexpected, witnesses naturally search for the closest available explanation, whether that comes from [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}), popular culture, aviation, religion or previous beliefs.

## Observation Versus Inference

Separating observation from inference is not about stripping away everything interesting. It is about identifying which parts of the account remain stable regardless of explanation.

For example, suppose two investigators examine the same report.

The witness says:

> "A silent triangular craft flew over my house."

One investigator immediately asks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How large did it appear?
* What colour were the lights?
* Which direction was it travelling?
* Approximately how long was it visible?
* Did it pass behind clouds?
* Could you hear traffic or wind at the same time?

</div>

These questions recover observations.

By contrast, questions such as "Did it seem extraterrestrial?" or "Was it cloaked?" encourage further interpretation rather than recovering perception.

Investigative interview methods such as the Cognitive Interview similarly encourage witnesses to report everything they remember without filtering details through assumptions about importance. ## Details That Survive Poor Viewing Conditions

Many strange-event reports occur under conditions that naturally reduce perceptual accuracy:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* darkness
* fog or rain
* long viewing distance
* brief exposure
* emotional surprise
* partial obstruction
* divided attention

</div>

These conditions do not automatically invalidate testimony. Instead, they determine which observations deserve greater confidence.

Certain features often remain relatively robust even under poor conditions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* approximate direction
* duration
* sequence of events
* relative movement
* number of sounds
* whether multiple objects were present
* whether the event was continuous or intermittent

</div>

Other details become much less reliable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* exact size
* precise distance
* facial features
* fine surface detail
* colour under poor lighting
* complex shape viewed briefly

</div>

A witness who honestly says, "I couldn't tell exactly what shape it had" is often preserving the integrity of the report rather than weakening it. Careful uncertainty is generally more valuable than confident embellishment.

## How Labels Can Outrun the Evidence

Human beings rapidly categorise unfamiliar experiences.

A witness who initially describes:

> "A bright orange light moving silently"

may later refer to it simply as:

> "the UFO"

The label becomes shorthand, but it also changes how the event is remembered and discussed.

This process can become stronger over time through:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* conversations with family or friends
* media reports
* documentaries
* internet discussions
* repeated interviews
* exposure to similar stories

</div>

Research on eyewitness memory demonstrates that later information can alter recollection, a phenomenon known as the misinformation effect. Repeated exposure to explanations can become incorporated into remembered experience even when the original observation was more limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misinformation_effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Misinformation effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation effect</span></span></span>

For Fortean researchers, this means that the earliest recorded account is often the most valuable. It is usually closest to the original perception and least influenced by subsequent interpretation.

## Practical Techniques for Preserving Observation

Investigators who specialise in unusual reports often use simple techniques that reduce interpretive contamination.

Instead of asking:

* "Was it hovering unnaturally?"

they ask:

* "Describe its movement."

Instead of:

* "Did the creature threaten you?"

they ask:

* "What did it do next?"

Instead of:

* "Did you think it was paranormal?"

they ask:

* "What made you think something unusual had happened?"

Useful prompts include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* "What happened first?"
* "What could you actually see?"
* "What could you hear?"
* "What made you notice it?"
* "What happened immediately afterwards?"
* "Which parts are you certain about?"
* "Which parts are your best guess?"

</div>

Notice that the final question explicitly separates memory from interpretation. Many witnesses readily distinguish between what they know and what they inferred once invited to do so.

## Why Honest Witnesses Still Add Interpretations

Interpretation is not a sign of deception. It is a normal feature of perception.

Psychologists describe perception as an active process in which the brain combines incoming sensory information with existing knowledge to construct a coherent understanding of events. This allows people to react quickly in everyday life but also means that unfamiliar events are interpreted through familiar mental models.

Someone unfamiliar with astronomical objects may interpret Venus as an approaching aircraft.

Someone familiar with UFO literature may interpret the same observation as an unidentified craft.

Someone raised with local folklore may identify an indistinct figure as a known legendary being.

The underlying observation may be identical while the interpretation differs dramatically.

For this reason, disagreement over explanations does not necessarily imply disagreement about what was actually observed.

## A Strong Strange-Event Report Keeps the Two Separate

The most valuable witness testimony often contains explicit distinctions such as:

> "I saw a bright object move silently across the sky. I don't know what it was."

or:

> "Something large crossed the road. I assumed it was an animal at first, but I couldn't identify the species."

These statements preserve both elements:

* the observation
* the witness's interpretation

Readers and investigators can then evaluate each independently.

That approach also allows new explanations to emerge without discarding the original testimony. A report first interpreted as paranormal may later match a rare atmospheric phenomenon, while another may remain unexplained because the observations resist ordinary identification. In either case, the preserved observations retain their evidential value, whereas unsupported labels can easily become obstacles to understanding.
