## Introduction

J. Allen Hynek's close encounter scale became influential not because it ranked [UFO reports]({{ 'ufo-reports/' | relative_url }}) from ordinary to extraordinary, but because it separated different kinds of claims that require different kinds of evidence. Rather than treating every sighting as a single mystery, Hynek divided reports by distance, observational conditions and the presence of physical effects or entities. That shift changed the investigative question. A distant light in the night sky, a radar-confirmed [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) and a reported landing with ground traces are not simply stronger or weaker versions of the same event—they are different evidential problems that call for different methods of verification. Hynek introduced this framework in *The UFO Experience* (1972), drawing on years of reviewing cases during and after his work with the US Air Force's UFO investigations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

Within [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), this approach illustrates how classification can preserve the strangeness of unusual reports while avoiding the mistake of judging every case by the same standards.

## Distant sightings versus close encounters

Hynek's system first separates observations made at a distance from those involving close proximity. The distinction is practical rather than dramatic.

His three categories for distant observations were:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Nocturnal Lights (NL):** unexplained lights seen at night.
* **Daylight Discs (DD):** structured objects observed during daylight, regardless of whether they were literally disc-shaped.
* **Radar-Visual (RV):** reports in which a visual observation was accompanied by radar detection.

</div>

Each category presents different opportunities—and limitations—for investigation. A nocturnal light may leave little beyond witness testimony and astronomical conditions. A daylight object may allow better estimates of shape, motion and duration. A radar-visual case introduces instrument data, although radar evidence itself requires careful scrutiny because atmospheric effects, equipment limitations and interpretation errors can all produce false returns. Hynek did not regard radar confirmation as automatic proof; instead, it represented an additional line of evidence that could be compared against eyewitness accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

Only after separating these distant observations did Hynek introduce the "close encounter" categories, generally applying to observations within roughly 500 feet (150 metres), where ordinary [misidentification]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) should, in principle, become less likely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

## What each class asks investigators to verify

The real value of the close encounter scale lies in the questions each category encourages investigators to ask.

**Close Encounter of the First Kind (CE1)** concerns a nearby observation with no claimed physical interaction. The primary task is to establish what was actually seen. Investigators compare witness descriptions, viewing conditions, duration, estimated distance, direction of travel and possible conventional explanations. Even at close range, perception can be unreliable, so consistency between independent witnesses becomes important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

**Close Encounter of the Second Kind (CE2)** introduces alleged physical effects. These may include ground impressions, scorched vegetation, electromagnetic interference, effects on vehicles or other measurable traces. Here the investigation shifts from eyewitness [psychology]({{ 'psychology/' | relative_url }}) towards physical evidence. Questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Can the trace be documented and dated?
* Is there a conventional environmental explanation?
* Was the evidence collected promptly enough to preserve its value?
* Do laboratory findings match the witness account?

</div>

Because physical traces can be independently examined, CE2 reports potentially allow evidence that survives beyond human memory, although many famous cases have ultimately proved ambiguous or poorly documented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

**Close Encounter of the Third Kind (CE3)** involves reports of apparent occupants or entities associated with the object. This does not simply add another observation; it introduces an entirely different evidential challenge. Investigators must now evaluate witness perception of living figures, behavioural descriptions, communication claims and possible psychological influences alongside the original sighting. Such reports often become difficult to corroborate because they depend heavily on testimony rather than durable physical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

The important point is that Hynek's categories do not imply increasing certainty. They identify increasing complexity in the evidence that must be evaluated.

## Why the scale improved UFO investigation

Hynek's classification helped move UFO investigation away from treating all reports as interchangeable anecdotes.

For example, if a researcher receives:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a brief report of an unusual light,
* a radar track with multiple observers,
* and a case involving alleged landing marks,

</div>

it makes little sense to evaluate all three using identical criteria. The first depends primarily on observational reliability. The second requires technical analysis of radar performance alongside eyewitness accounts. The third calls for site examination, photography, sampling and documentation before environmental changes erase possible evidence.

This distinction also improves statistical work. Researchers can compare similar reports rather than mixing fundamentally different phenomena into a single database. Later investigators, including Allan Hendry at the Center for UFO Studies, used Hynek's categories while examining how many reported UFOs could ultimately be identified as aircraft, astronomical objects or other conventional phenomena. Classification made those comparisons more meaningful because similar cases were grouped together before analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identification_studies_of_UFOs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Identification studies of UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Identification studies of UFOs</span></span></span>

In this sense, Hynek's system functions less like a theory of UFOs than like a filing system for evidence.

## Where the scale helps and where it strains

The classification remains widely recognised because it is intuitive and easy to apply, but researchers have long noted its limitations.

One difficulty is that reports often fit more than one category. An object might first appear as a distant daylight sighting before approaching close enough to qualify as a close encounter. A radar-visual event could also involve alleged physical traces. The boundaries are therefore not always clean.

Another criticism is that the distant categories classify the observer's [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}) rather than the reported behaviour of the phenomenon. Jacques Vallée later argued that this makes computer-based comparison difficult because descriptions such as "disc", "light" or "triangle" depend partly on witness perception. He proposed behavioural classifications that focus on what the reported object did rather than how it appeared. Even so, Vallée retained Hynek's close encounter categories because they remained useful for distinguishing reports involving physical traces or entities from simple observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufo-com.net/_pics/vik_gajd/vallee_sysclassif.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufo-com.net">[ufo-com.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufo-com.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nd radar-visual cases, Hynek&#x27;s close encounter designations: AN1 events are anomalies like amorphous...</span></span></span>

The familiar "fourth" and "fifth" close encounters were also not part of Hynek's original 1972 scheme. Later writers introduced additional categories, particularly for abduction narratives and other claimed interactions, but these extensions are not universally accepted and reflect different theoretical assumptions from Hynek's original evidence-oriented framework.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span>

## Why the scale still matters in Forteana

Within Fortean studies, Hynek's scale demonstrates that classification need not diminish mystery. Instead, it separates questions before attempting answers.

A report of unexplained lights asks whether observers accurately described an aerial event. A radar-visual case asks whether independent instruments support the observation. A landing-trace case asks whether physical evidence survives examination. An entity report asks whether extraordinary testimony can be corroborated in ways beyond narrative consistency.

Those are not different degrees of the same puzzle. They are different puzzles altogether. By recognising that distinction, Hynek's classification remains one of the clearest examples of how a Fortean taxonomy can organise anomalous reports without assuming they all share a single cause—or deserve the same evidential weight.
