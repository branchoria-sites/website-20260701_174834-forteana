## Introduction

Reports of fish or frogs apparently falling from the sky are among the best-known examples in Fortean literature, but they are also some of the most approachable for practical investigation. While such events can seem extraordinary, meteorologists have long proposed mechanisms that can account for at least some cases, especially where severe [weather]({{ 'weather/' | relative_url }}), [waterspouts]({{ 'waterspouts/' | relative_url }}) or flooding are involved. The key lesson for a beginner is that an unusual outcome does not automatically require an unusual cause. Before treating an animal fall as a mystery, investigate the weather, the landscape and the animals themselves.

For fieldworkers, these reports are valuable because they combine eyewitness testimony with physical evidence that can often be examined. The distribution of the animals, their condition, recent weather records and nearby water sources may reveal whether a storm transported them, floodwater deposited them or another process created the illusion of a "rain" of animals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/everyday-mysteries/meteorology-climatology/item/can-it-rain-frogs-fish-and-other-objects/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">can it rain frogs fish and other objects</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCan it rain frogs, fish, and other objects?Many scientists believe tornadic waterspouts may be responsible for fro...</span></span></span>

## What witnesses usually report

Accounts of animal falls share several recurring features despite occurring in different countries and different centuries.

Witnesses commonly describe:

* Fish, frogs or tadpoles appearing immediately after heavy rain or thunderstorms.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://earthsky.org/earth/raining-fish-and-frogs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earthsky.org">[earthsky.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earthsky.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">raining fish and frogs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">They may pick up fish or frogs or other small creatures, carry them some distance and then drop...Read more...</span></span></span>
* Animals concentrated within a surprisingly small area rather than spread across an entire town.
* Only [one species]({{ 'one-species/' | relative_url }}) being present, often individuals of similar size.
* Some animals still alive and active after landing.
* Little or no obvious source of the animals nearby.

These details matter because they help distinguish between several different mechanisms. A concentrated patch of small fish after a violent storm suggests a different process from frogs appearing across flooded gardens over several hours.

Historical reports include repeated fish falls in Honduras, often known as *lluvia de peces*, and numerous documented fish and frog falls in Europe, North America and Asia. Although these events have become part of Fortean [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}), many individual cases remain poorly documented beyond newspaper accounts, making contemporary field investigation especially valuable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/everyday-mysteries/meteorology-climatology/item/can-it-rain-frogs-fish-and-other-objects/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">can it rain frogs fish and other objects</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCan it rain frogs, fish, and other objects?Many scientists believe tornadic waterspouts may be responsible for fro...</span></span></span>

## Waterspouts and storm explanations

The leading meteorological explanation involves waterspouts and, less commonly, tornadoes crossing lakes, rivers or coastal waters.

A waterspout is a rotating column of air over water. Strong tornadic waterspouts are capable of lifting spray, lightweight debris and, potentially, small aquatic animals into the circulation. Once carried aloft, fish or amphibians may be transported some distance before falling when the storm weakens or changes structure. Meteorologists consider this physically plausible, although direct [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) of animals being lifted and later dropped is extremely rare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/everyday-mysteries/meteorology-climatology/item/can-it-rain-frogs-fish-and-other-objects/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">can it rain frogs fish and other objects</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCan it rain frogs, fish, and other objects?Many scientists believe tornadic waterspouts may be responsible for fro...</span></span></span>

* Most reported animals are small and lightweight.
* Many reports follow thunderstorms or severe weather.
* Fish and frogs are often associated with shallow water where they are easier to lift.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://earthsky.org/earth/raining-fish-and-frogs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earthsky.org">[earthsky.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earthsky.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">raining fish and frogs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">They may pick up fish or frogs or other small creatures, carry them some distance and then drop...Read more...</span></span></span>

## When "animal rain" is not actually rain

One of the most useful habits in [Fortean fieldwork]({{ 'fieldwork/' | relative_url }}) is to ask whether anything truly fell from the sky at all.

Heavy rainfall and flash flooding can wash fish from overflowing ponds, streams or drainage channels into roads, gardens and fields. When the water rapidly drains away, observers may encounter scattered fish with no obvious explanation, especially if they did not witness the flooding itself.

Frogs provide another example. During wet weather, many amphibians emerge simultaneously to migrate or breed. Large numbers appearing suddenly after rain can create the impression that they have fallen from above when they have actually emerged from nearby vegetation, ditches or temporary pools. Similar processes have been suggested for some historical reports involving worms, snails and other small animals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://modernfarmer.com/2014/03/things-fall-sky/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: modernfarmer.com">[Modern Farmer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">modernfarmer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">things fall sky</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Modern FarmerWhen Animals Fall from the Sky18 Mar 2014 — But what about the fish and frogs? “It is certainly within the realm of possibil...</span></span></span>

Birds can also produce misleading evidence. Predatory birds and seabirds occasionally drop fish in flight, either accidentally or when disturbed. Localised falls of damaged fish during otherwise calm weather have been linked to this behaviour rather than storms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://earthsky.org/earth/raining-fish-and-frogs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earthsky.org">[EarthSky]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earthsky.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">raining fish and frogs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">They may pick up fish or frogs or other small creatures, carry them some distance and then drop...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Evidence to collect at the scene

A careful investigation begins before the animals are disturbed or removed.

Record the following as quickly as possible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* The precise location using a map reference or GPS.
* The date and exact time of discovery.
* Recent weather, including thunderstorms, hail, tornado warnings or waterspout reports.
* The size of the affected area.
* Whether the animals are alive, injured or dead.
* Whether all belong to one species and similar age.
* Nearby ponds, rivers, drainage ditches, reservoirs or flooded ground.
* Photographs showing the original distribution before collection.

</div>

Collect environmental information alongside biological evidence. Weather radar archives, local meteorological observations and storm reports may indicate whether a rotating storm passed nearby. Likewise, checking local flood reports may reveal an overlooked source for displaced animals. If practical, identify the species. Knowing whether fish normally inhabit a nearby pond or whether the frogs are juvenile individuals emerging from a breeding site can greatly narrow the possibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/everyday-mysteries/meteorology-climatology/item/can-it-rain-frogs-fish-and-other-objects/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">can it rain frogs fish and other objects</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressCan it rain frogs, fish, and other objects?Many scientists believe tornadic waterspouts may be responsible for fro...</span></span></span>

## How to assess competing explanations

Rather than asking, "Could this be paranormal?", a Fortean investigator should compare competing explanations against the available evidence.

Questions worth asking include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was severe weather documented in the relevant place and time?
* Is there a plausible water source within the transport distance?
* Does the species naturally occur locally?
* Are the animals clean, muddy or physically damaged?
* Is there independent confirmation from multiple witnesses?
* Does the reported distribution match aerial deposition or ground transport?

</div>

The strongest cases are those with independent witnesses, preserved specimens, precise weather records and clear timelines. Conversely, stories repeated years later without documentation should be treated cautiously, regardless of how dramatic they sound.

## What these cases teach beginners

Fish and frog falls illustrate an important principle of Fortean investigation: unusual observations deserve careful documentation, not automatic [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}) or automatic belief.

Modern meteorology provides credible mechanisms that can explain many reported animal falls, particularly those associated with waterspouts, tornadoes and flooding. At the same time, not every individual case has enough evidence to identify a single cause with confidence. For beginners, the lesson is practical rather than philosophical. Record the weather, map the surroundings, identify the animals and preserve the earliest testimony before deciding whether a report represents a rare but natural event, a misunderstood local occurrence or a genuinely unresolved case.
