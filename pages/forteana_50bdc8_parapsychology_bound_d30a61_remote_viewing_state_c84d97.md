## Introduction

Remote viewing is one of the most unusual episodes in the history of [parapsychology]({{ 'parapsychology/' | relative_url }}) because it briefly moved from the margins of Fortean speculation into the centre of government-funded research. The claim was straightforward but extraordinary: that some people could describe distant or hidden locations, objects or events without using the ordinary senses. During the Cold War, this idea attracted enough attention within the United States intelligence community to receive sustained funding, classified oversight and operational testing. Yet the same programme ultimately became a case study in how governments evaluate extraordinary claims, weighing statistical anomalies against practical usefulness and scientific credibility. Rather than proving or disproving psychic abilities once and for all, the history of remote viewing reveals how national-security institutions sometimes investigate unlikely ideas when the perceived strategic stakes appear high.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMUtts and Hyman were asked to prepare independent reports based on their review. ## What remote viewing claimed to test

The term "remote viewing" emerged in the early 1970s during research led by physicist Harold Puthoff and engineer Russell Targ at the Stanford Research Institute (now SRI International). They deliberately avoided older terms such as "clairvoyance" or "ESP", hoping to frame the work as a testable information-gathering process rather than a spiritual or occult practice. Remote viewers were asked to describe a distant target—perhaps a building, landscape or object—without any normal sensory access to it. Their verbal descriptions and sketches were then compared with the actual target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_viewing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Remote viewing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Remote viewing</span></span></span>

Researchers attempted to reduce opportunities for fraud or sensory leakage by introducing blind protocols. In stronger experimental designs, neither the participant nor the experimenter interacting with them knew the target during the session. Independent judges later matched the descriptions against multiple possible targets to determine whether performance exceeded chance expectations. Supporters argued that repeated statistical departures from chance suggested an anomalous source of information, even if no accepted physical mechanism could explain it. Critics responded that methodological weaknesses, subtle cueing, selective reporting and subjective judging could generate apparently impressive results without requiring paranormal explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMUtts and Hyman were asked to prepare independent reports based on their review. In this rev...</span></span></span>

The resulting debate shifted away from spectacular anecdotes and towards questions familiar to experimental science: Were the protocols genuinely blind? Could independent laboratories reproduce the findings? Were statistical effects large enough to matter in practical applications?

## Why governments became interested

Remote viewing reached government attention largely because of Cold War uncertainty. During the 1970s, reports circulated that the Soviet Union was investing resources in psychic research with possible military applications. Whether those reports accurately reflected Soviet capabilities mattered less than the possibility that an adversary might discover an unexpected intelligence advantage. Intelligence agencies have historically explored unconventional technologies whenever the potential strategic payoff appears large enough, even if the probability of success seems low.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu/olj/sa/sa_jan02srm01.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu">[ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ere trained to acquire such &#x27;Remote Viewing&#x27; capabilities for collecting...Read more...</span></span></span>

This climate led to a succession of classified American programmes that evolved through several organisational names before becoming known collectively as the Stargate Project. Funding and management shifted among military and intelligence organisations over roughly two decades, while research moved from SRI to Science Applications International Corporation (SAIC). Investigators explored both controlled laboratory experiments and operational tasks in which remote viewers attempted to describe foreign facilities, hostages, missing aircraft or other intelligence targets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMUtts and Hyman were asked to prepare independent reports based on their review. The programme's existence demonstrates that governments do not require certainty before investigating unconventional ideas. Intelligence work routinely deals with incomplete information, and agencies sometimes pursue speculative research if the expected cost is modest relative to a potentially transformative capability. Remote viewing therefore became a policy problem rather than merely a scientific curiosity.

## From laboratory experiment to intelligence programme

Operational use proved far more demanding than statistical research. A laboratory experiment only needed to demonstrate performance above chance under controlled conditions. Intelligence agencies required information that was timely, specific, accurate and reliable enough to influence real decisions.

Former participants described attempts to integrate remote-viewing reports into broader intelligence assessments rather than treating them as stand-alone evidence. In practice, however, the material often consisted of symbolic impressions, partial sketches and ambiguous descriptions requiring substantial interpretation. This ambiguity made it difficult to distinguish genuine successes from retrospective matching, in which vague statements appear meaningful only after the true outcome becomes known.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMUtts and Hyman were asked to prepare independent reports based on their review. The gap between statistical significance and operational usefulness became increasingly important. Even if an experiment suggested performance slightly above chance across many trials, intelligence officers needed dependable information on individual missions. An intelligence method that occasionally produces intriguing results but cannot reliably indicate when it is correct has limited operational value.

## Why the programme ended

In 1995, responsibility for the programme shifted to the CIA, which commissioned the American Institutes for Research (AIR) to conduct an independent evaluation. The review became notable because it deliberately included experts representing contrasting positions on parapsychology.

Jessica Utts, a statistician, concluded that the accumulated experimental evidence indicated a genuine anomaly that could not easily be dismissed as chance or methodological error. She argued that the statistical effects deserved continued scientific investigation. Ray Hyman, a psychologist and long-time critic of paranormal claims, accepted that some experiments appeared stronger than many earlier parapsychology studies but argued that the evidence remained insufficient to establish psychic functioning because independent replication, theoretical understanding and methodological certainty were still lacking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA+2CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMUtts and Hyman were asked to prepare independent reports based on their review. The broader AIR assessment focused on a different question from the existence of psi itself: whether the programme had produced useful intelligence. Its conclusion was largely negative. Reviewers found no persuasive evidence that remote viewing had generated operationally valuable intelligence beyond what conventional methods could provide, and they recommended ending the programme. The CIA subsequently terminated it and declassified much of its documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMUtts and Hyman were asked to prepare independent reports based on their review. ## What the controversy says about evidence standards

Remote viewing remains significant because it exposes a tension between two different standards of evidence.

The first concerns scientific inference. Some researchers argued that repeated statistical deviations from chance, if obtained under sufficiently rigorous controls, justify investigating an unknown phenomenon even without an accepted explanatory theory. Others maintained that extraordinary claims require especially robust replication and elimination of every plausible conventional explanation before they should be accepted.

The second concerns policy. Governments rarely fund research solely because something is scientifically interesting. Intelligence agencies ultimately ask whether a method improves decisions under real-world conditions. A technique can produce statistically unusual results yet still fail operationally if it lacks consistency, precision or predictability. ## Remote viewing within Forteana

Within the wider landscape of [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), remote viewing occupies an unusual position. Most Fortean subjects remain collections of anecdotes, [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}), witness testimony and unresolved mysteries. Remote viewing instead generated classified budgets, formal protocols, statistical analyses, peer-reviewed publications and government oversight. It therefore crossed an institutional boundary that very few anomalous claims have crossed.

That transition did not produce scientific consensus or enduring intelligence capability. Instead, it transformed remote viewing into a lasting case study of how extraordinary claims interact with bureaucracy, secrecy and evidence-based decision-making. Its legacy lies less in proving psychic perception than in demonstrating how states sometimes investigate highly unconventional ideas when strategic uncertainty is high—and how such investigations are ultimately judged not only by intriguing results but by reliability, reproducibility and practical usefulness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMUtts and Hyman were asked to prepare independent reports based on their review.
