## Introduction

Within [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})'s *The Book of the Damned*, unexplained [disappearances]({{ 'disappearances/' | relative_url }}) occupy a distinctive place. Unlike accounts of strange objects falling from the sky or mysterious lights, disappearances unsettle because the anomaly is human. A missing person leaves behind interrupted routines, uncertain witnesses, incomplete paperwork and emotional consequences that cannot easily be filed away. Fort used such reports not primarily to prove extraordinary forces were at work, but to demonstrate how unresolved events expose the limits of tidy explanations and institutional certainty. In the Fortean tradition, vanishings become "damned facts": cases that remain awkward because neither conventional explanations nor paranormal speculation can fully account for them. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">His quarrel with science centers on its failure to recognize the universe&#x27;s wholeness. ## Disappearance stories in Fort's range

Fort did not develop a systematic theory of missing persons. Instead, he assembled reports from [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}), official publications and historical accounts, treating them as part of a larger collection of anomalies that resisted comfortable classification. His catalogue included people allegedly disappearing under unusual circumstances, maritime mysteries such as the *Mary Celeste* (which he examined more extensively in later work), and historical episodes where records themselves appeared confused or contradictory. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_Damned" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Book of the Damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Book of the Damned</span></span></span>

This method mattered more than any individual case. Rather than arguing that every disappearance represented supernatural intervention, Fort repeatedly juxtaposed:

* isolated newspaper reports;
* conflicting witness accounts;
* incomplete official investigations;
* historical incidents preserved only through secondary [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}).

The accumulation created a different kind of evidence. Readers encountered not a single compelling mystery but a pattern of unresolved documentation. The anomaly often lay as much in the surviving record as in the disappearance itself. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">His quarrel with science centers on its failure to recognize the universe&#x27;s wholeness. Scientists...</span></span></span>

Fort's treatment therefore differs from later paranormal literature that attempts to identify one hidden cause—whether extraterrestrial, cryptid or occult. His concern was that reality continually produced cases that neither certainty nor [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}) handled well.

## Why witnesses become part of the anomaly

A striking feature of Fortean disappearances is that witnesses frequently become as important as the missing individual.

When someone vanishes unexpectedly, the surviving evidence is almost entirely human:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* memories that change over time;
* contradictory statements;
* rumours spreading beyond verified facts;
* journalists compressing complex events into dramatic narratives;
* investigators forced to work with incomplete information.

</div>

Fort recognised that these secondary effects deserve attention because they reveal how anomalies develop socially. The disappearance itself may remain unexplained, but the changing story becomes observable.

This emphasis distinguishes Fortean thinking from straightforward mystery-solving. Instead of asking only, "What happened?", Fort implicitly asks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which details survived?
* Which details disappeared?
* Who decided which version became accepted?
* What evidence was quietly discarded?

</div>

That focus reflects his broader criticism that institutions routinely classify inconvenient information as error or irrelevance before fully understanding it. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">His quarrel with science centers on its failure to recognize the universe&#x27;s wholeness. ## Missing records can be as important as missing people

One recurring Fortean theme is the disappearance of documentation itself.

Historical vanishings often rely upon:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* fragmented archives;
* newspaper summaries rather than original testimony;
* missing official files;
* contradictory dates;
* repeated retellings that gradually acquire new details.

</div>

From a conventional historical perspective, these weaknesses reduce reliability. Fort acknowledged such weaknesses but also regarded them as worthy of study because they illustrate how evidence is preserved unevenly.

A disappearance may therefore remain mysterious for two different reasons:

1. **The original event was genuinely unusual.**
2. **The surviving record became damaged, selective or incomplete.**

Fort resisted collapsing these possibilities into one answer. The uncertainty itself becomes part of the phenomenon. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">His quarrel with science centers on its failure to recognize the universe&#x27;s wholeness. ## The human stakes of anomaly collecting

Disappearances occupy a special place within [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}) because they involve irreversible human absence rather than unusual natural events.

A rain of frogs or an unexplained light can be discussed with curiosity. A missing person immediately introduces grief, fear and unresolved responsibility.

This changes the emotional character of anomaly collecting. The questions become practical as well as philosophical:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the person ever found?
* Did authorities investigate adequately?
* Were witnesses believed?
* Did rumours overwhelm evidence?
* Can later researchers reconstruct what actually happened?

</div>

Fort generally avoided exploiting tragedy for emotional effect. Instead, disappearances reinforced his argument that certainty is often claimed before the available evidence justifies it. When official explanations appeared incomplete, or when no explanation emerged at all, the unresolved case exposed the limits of confident classification rather than proving extraordinary causes. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">His quarrel with science centers on its failure to recognize the universe&#x27;s wholeness. ## Why Fortean vanishings feel different

Many traditions contain stories of people who disappear mysteriously. Fortean disappearances differ because they are presented as problems of evidence rather than [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}) alone.

Several characteristics recur:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ordinary settings.** The missing person often disappears during everyday activity rather than within a mythic landscape.
* **Documentary traces.** Newspaper reports, official correspondence and witness testimony remain central.
* **Persistent uncertainty.** Neither sceptical nor paranormal explanations completely resolve every detail.
* **Attention to process.** Researchers examine how reports circulate, change and are forgotten as much as the disappearance itself.

</div>

This makes Fortean vanishings intellectually different from ghost stories or legends. Their power lies in occupying an uncomfortable middle ground where documentation exists but remains incomplete.

## Continuing influence on Fortean investigation

Later Fortean writers inherited Fort's attitude more than his individual cases. Modern collections of unexplained disappearances frequently compare reports across decades while remaining alert to mundane explanations such as accidents, reporting errors, deliberate disappearances or flawed journalism alongside genuinely unresolved elements.

That balanced posture reflects Fort's idea of "intermediatism": resisting premature certainty in either direction. A disappearance is neither automatically paranormal nor automatically solved simply because a plausible explanation exists. The surviving evidence must be weighed on its own merits, recognising both the limits of historical records and the human tendency to construct satisfying narratives around unresolved events. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">His quarrel with science centers on its failure to recognize the universe&#x27;s wholeness.
