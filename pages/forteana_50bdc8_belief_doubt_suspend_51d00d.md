## Introduction

[Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}) works best when belief and disbelief are both kept provisional. A Fortean case usually begins with a report that is strange enough to resist easy filing: a light in the sky, a creature seen at the edge of visibility, a fall of animals, a haunting, a coincidence that feels engineered. The weakest responses are instant belief, which turns mystery into doctrine, and instant [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}), which turns scepticism into a reflex. Suspended judgment is the more useful middle practice: take the report seriously enough to investigate, but not so seriously that the preferred explanation outruns the evidence.

That stance fits the tradition [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }}) helped create. In *[The Book]({{ 'the-book/' | relative_url }}) of the Damned*, Fort described his subject as “excluded” data: observations and reports pushed outside accepted categories. His provocation was not simply that science was wrong and marvels were true; it was that systems of knowledge can decide too quickly what belongs and what does not. The modern Fortean task is to keep that irritation alive without mistaking every rejected claim for a hidden truth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/22472/22472-h/22472-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Book of the DamnedTHE BOOK OF THE DAMNED. 1. ## Why suspended judgment is the Fortean default

Suspended judgment is not indecision for its own sake. It is a practical response to reports whose evidence is incomplete, mixed, degraded, second-hand, emotionally charged or difficult to reproduce. In ordinary life, people often ask whether a strange claim is “true or false”. Forteana usually requires a prior question: what kind of claim is this, and what would count as a responsible level of confidence?

Anomalistics, the organised study of anomalies, is useful here because it treats unusual reports as investigable without assuming that they must be paranormal. The Psi Encyclopedia describes anomalistics as multidisciplinary: an alleged UFO might require psychology, astronomy, physics, aircraft recognition, social history or several of these at once. It also notes that anomalistics is governed by scientific norms including scepticism, disinterestedness and openness to original data. That combination is close to the Fortean ideal: curiosity with brakes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/anomalistics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Anomalistics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaAnomalistics - Psi Encyclopedia13 Apr 2015 — Anomalistics is generally governed by the Mertonian norms of science: commun...</span></span></span>

The point is not to split the difference between believers and debunkers. Some claims are false. Some are probably true but misdescribed. Some are unresolved only because crucial data were never collected. Some may mark rare natural events, hoaxes, memory errors, folklore patterns or genuine gaps in present knowledge. Suspended judgment is the discipline of not letting any one of those possibilities become the conclusion before the case has earned it.

A helpful Fortean question is therefore not “Do I believe this?” but “What position does the evidence justify today?” That position may be strong acceptance, strong rejection, tentative explanation, open uncertainty, or simple archival interest. The value lies in matching confidence to evidence.

## The danger of instant belief

Instant belief is tempting because Fortean material often arrives with a strong human charge. A witness seems sincere. A photograph looks uncanny. A local tradition has lasted for decades. A scientific institution appears evasive. The story feels more vivid than the explanation. In those conditions, belief can feel like respect: respect for the witness, for the mystery, or for possibilities that official culture has neglected.

The problem is that sincerity is not the same as accuracy. Anomalistic psychology, the branch of psychology that examines unusual experiences and paranormal beliefs through known psychological and physical factors, gives Fortean readers a useful caution. Chris French, associated with Goldsmiths and its Anomalistic Psychology Research Unit, is described by Goldsmiths as specialising in non-paranormal explanations for ostensibly paranormal experiences. That framing matters because it does not require witnesses to be stupid, dishonest or attention-seeking. It treats error as a normal feature of perception, memory and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gold.ac.uk/psychology/staff/french/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gold.ac.uk">[Goldsmiths, University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gold.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">smiths, University of LondonChristopher FrenchChris is an expert in the area of anomalistic psychology, focusing upon non- paranormal...</span></span></span>

Research on memory and paranormal belief supports that caution. A study using conjuring to examine memory distortion notes that paranormal belief and reports of anomalous experiences have been linked with susceptibility to false memories, suggesting that some strange reports may arise from ordinary memory processes rather than deliberate invention. This is especially important in Forteana, where many famous cases survive through retelling, interviews, newspaper summaries and retrospective accounts rather than through fresh, instrumented observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4230037/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magic and memory: using conjuring to explore the effects of...by K Wilson · 2014 · Cited by 44 — A number of individual difference me...</span></span></span>

Instant belief also narrows the investigation. Once a haunting is treated as a ghost, the draught, floor vibration, sleep state, grief context, hoax possibility and local legend may receive less attention. Once a light in the sky is treated as an alien craft, Venus, aircraft, balloons, rockets, atmospheric effects and military activity become “debunking” rather than ordinary lines of inquiry. The believer may still gather evidence, but the case has already been sorted into a meaning.

In Fortean terms, this is a loss. The richest cases are often interesting precisely because they sit at the junction of witness experience, cultural expectation, environmental detail and evidential ambiguity. Overbelief flattens that junction into a single answer.

## The danger of instant dismissal

Instant dismissal is the mirror-image failure. It protects the reader from gullibility, but it can also protect them from information. A strange report may be poorly evidenced, but that does not mean it has no value. It may preserve a rare weather event, a misidentified astronomical object, a social panic, a local rumour system, an institutional blind spot or a witness’s honest attempt to describe something unfamiliar.

Fort’s anger was directed at exclusion that happens too confidently. His “damned” facts were not necessarily facts in the modern evidential sense; many were newspaper clippings, odd scientific notes and reports of uncertain quality. But his broader challenge remains useful: when an institution or expert community rejects a report, it is worth asking whether the rejection follows from careful analysis, lack of evidence, category prejudice, reputational anxiety or mere impatience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/22472/22472-h/22472-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Book of the DamnedTHE BOOK OF THE DAMNED. 1. A procession of the damned. By the damned, I mean the excluded. We shal...</span></span></span>

Marcello Truzzi, a sociologist associated with scientific scepticism and anomalistics, is helpful here. He is widely linked to the phrase that extraordinary claims require extraordinary proof, but his view of scepticism was not simply “reject the weird”. In his essay on pseudo-scepticism, Truzzi argued that the burden of proof falls on the claimant and that more extraordinary claims carry a heavier burden, while also warning against sceptics who make confident negative claims without meeting their own burden.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.anomalist.com/commentaries/pseudo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anomalist.com">[anomalist.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anomalist.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on anomalist.com.</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to good Fortean method. “Not proved” is not the same as “disproved”. “Probably misidentified” is not the same as “the witness lied”. “No reliable evidence for a paranormal explanation” is not the same as “nothing happened”. The best scepticism is not contempt; it is disciplined proportion.

[Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book-75a50c/' | relative_url }}), the United States Air Force’s UFO investigation programme, shows why this matters. The National Archives notes that the project’s records were declassified, that the project closed in 1969, and that its files are available for examination. A Project Blue Book document distinguishes identified reports from cases with insufficient data, defining insufficient-data cases as those missing essential elements such as duration, date, time, location, weather conditions or manner of appearance. That is a useful model for Fortean doubt: some cases are not solved; they are under-described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

## How uncertainty becomes honest rather than evasive

Suspended judgment is sometimes confused with a vague refusal to decide. In Forteana, it should be more exact than that. Honest uncertainty names what is known, what is inferred, what is missing and what would change the assessment.

A practical Fortean reading of a strange case can sort claims into several levels:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Reported experience:** what the witness or source says happened.
* **Recorded details:** time, place, weather, direction, duration, documents, images, physical traces and independent accounts.
* **Interpretation:** what the witness thought the event was.
* **Alternative explanations:** ordinary causes that fit some or all of the details.
* **Residual uncertainty:** what remains unclear after the strongest explanations have been tested.
* **Confidence level:** how far the evidence justifies belief, doubt or continued suspension.

</div>

This separation prevents a common mistake: treating doubt about the interpretation as dismissal of the experience. A person may genuinely have seen a bright moving light and still be wrong that it was a spacecraft. A household may genuinely have heard frightening night sounds and still be wrong that the cause was a ghost. A community may genuinely preserve an old monster story and still be preserving folklore rather than zoology.

The former US president Jimmy Carter’s UFO report is a useful example of this distinction. Public accounts of the incident note that Carter described seeing an unidentified object in 1969 but did not conclude that it was an extraterrestrial craft. Later discussion proposed explanations including Venus or a high-altitude barium cloud linked to atmospheric research. The lesson is not that every famous sighting has a tidy solution. It is that “unidentified” is a status of information, not a licence to insert the most exciting explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter_UFO_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jimmy Carter UFO incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jimmy Carter UFO incident</span></span></span>

The same logic applies outside UFOs. In cryptid cases, for example, the strongest question is rarely “Could unknown animals exist?” In principle, yes: new species are found, and eyewitness testimony sometimes begins real inquiry. The harder question is whether a specific claim has moved beyond anecdote, folklore, poor photographs and ambiguous tracks. Reviews and interviews around Daniel Loxton and Donald Prothero’s *Abominable Science!* present cryptozoology as a field where sceptical examination can educate readers about both monster traditions and evidence standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/676115" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.uchicago.edu">[Chicago Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uchicago.edu.</span></span></span>

## Belief, doubt and the burden of proof

The burden of proof is not an anti-Fortean rule. It is what makes Fortean inquiry possible. Without it, every possible explanation competes on equal terms: ghost, hoax, hallucination, magnetic anomaly, alien craft, misremembered dream, rare animal, atmospheric effect. Equal possibility is not open-mindedness; it is evidential collapse.

The phrase “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence” is often used in debates about paranormal claims. It has roots in earlier arguments about miracles and was popularised in modern sceptical culture through figures including Carl Sagan, while Truzzi used a closely related formulation. The useful version is not that strange claims are forbidden. It is that claims which overturn well-supported background knowledge need enough evidence to justify that overturning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraordinary_claims_require_extraordinary_evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence</span></span></span>

There is a danger, however, in using the phrase as a slogan. If “extraordinary evidence” means “evidence I will never accept”, then it becomes a shield for orthodoxy rather than a standard of inquiry. Critics of the phrase have argued that its meaning can be subjective, and more recent sceptical discussion has warned that repeating it as a reflex can be counterproductive in practical investigations. The better Fortean version is plainer: the more a claim asks us to revise, the more carefully we should test it, and the more independently the evidence should converge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraordinary_claims_require_extraordinary_evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence</span></span></span>

That standard protects both sides. It prevents believers from treating one witness, one blur or one coincidence as world-changing proof. It also prevents sceptics from dismissing a case merely because it is unfashionable, embarrassing or associated with paranormal culture. Strong claims need strong support; strong rejections need real engagement with the evidence.

## A Fortean method for holding doubt

Suspended judgment becomes useful when it changes behaviour. It should make the investigator slower to label, more careful with sources and more explicit about uncertainty. A Fortean reader does not need a laboratory to practise this; most of the work is disciplined attention.

Start with the earliest available version of the report. Later tellings often grow cleaner, stranger or more symbolic. Newspaper [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}), podcast summaries and social media posts may add drama while dropping dates, distances, weather and source qualifications. Fort himself relied heavily on collected reports, which is part of his fascination and part of the reason modern readers must treat his archive as a starting point rather than a verdict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/22472/22472-h/22472-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Book of the DamnedTHE BOOK OF THE DAMNED. 1. A procession of the damned. By the damned, I mean the excluded. We shal...</span></span></span>

Then separate the anomaly from the proposed meaning. “A luminous object was seen for three minutes in the western sky” is a different claim from “an alien craft hovered over the town”. “Several people heard knocks in the walls” is a different claim from “a poltergeist responded intelligently”. “A large dark animal crossed the road” is a different claim from “a surviving prehistoric creature lives in the area”. The first claim may deserve investigation even when the second is weak.

Next, look for what would normally be expected if the favoured explanation were true. A physical creature should leave ecological pressure, repeated traces, plausible habitat and eventually better images or remains. A recurring aerial phenomenon should correlate with flight paths, astronomical conditions, launch activity or instrument records. A haunting claim may need attention to building acoustics, sleep disruption, carbon monoxide risk, suggestion, family stress and local legend before extraordinary explanations are entertained.

Finally, state the current position without inflation. “Unexplained from the available record” is often the most honest answer. It does not mean paranormal. It does not mean solved. It means the evidence has not earned either a confident ordinary explanation or a confident extraordinary one.

## What suspended judgment is not

Suspended judgment is not the same as giving every claim endless attention. Some reports are too vague to carry weight. Some sources are known hoaxes. Some claims conflict so strongly with established evidence that only exceptionally robust new evidence would make them live possibilities. Good Fortean doubt includes the right to stop when a case has no usable trail.

Nor is suspended judgment a posture of permanent mystery. Forteana often attracts people who enjoy unresolved stories, but an explanation does not ruin a case. A solved case can still be culturally revealing. A misidentified planet can teach perception. A hoax can teach media appetite. A rumour can teach local fear. A false monster can teach how landscapes become storied. The aim is not to preserve mystery at any cost; it is to preserve inquiry long enough for the right kind of understanding to emerge.

Suspended judgment also does not require emotional neutrality. Witnesses may be frightened, grieving, exhilarated or sincere. Investigators may feel wonder, suspicion or fascination. The discipline is not to eliminate those responses but to stop them from doing the evidential work.

## Why this matters beyond strange stories

Fortean suspended judgment has become more useful in the internet age. Strange claims now circulate through short clips, decontextualised documents, AI-generated images, old cases reposted as new, and communities that reward both credulity and aggressive debunking. The old Fortean problem — how to think about reports that do not fit — has become a general media problem.

The best response is neither to believe everything nor to sneer at everything. It is to cultivate proportionate confidence. That means respecting testimony without treating it as final, valuing scepticism without turning it into contempt, and recognising that “unexplained” is a temporary evidential category rather than a destination.

Forteana is most valuable when it keeps the mind in motion. Belief asks what might be possible. Doubt asks what has actually been shown. Suspended judgment lets both questions remain alive long enough for the case to become clearer.

    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
