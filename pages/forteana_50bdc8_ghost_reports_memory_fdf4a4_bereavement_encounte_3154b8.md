## Introduction

Bereavement ghost experiences differ from the classic haunted-house story because they are centred on a relationship rather than a location. Instead of recurring phenomena tied to a building, they typically involve a recently deceased family member, partner or close friend whose presence is sensed during the grieving process. Reports range from vividly seeing the deceased, hearing their voice or smelling a familiar perfume to simply feeling that they are nearby. For many people these experiences are comforting rather than frightening, and they often become part of family memory instead of public ghost lore.

Within Fortean studies, bereavement encounters occupy an unusual position. They are among the most frequently reported forms of apparent ghost [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}), yet they are also among the least compatible with simple "haunting" narratives. Psychological research, anthropology and psychical research all recognise that such experiences are common, sincere and personally meaningful, while disagreeing over whether they represent grief-related perception, an aspect of continuing emotional bonds, or evidence for survival after death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 106 — Bereavement among elderly people: grief...</span></span></span>

## What bereavement encounters usually feel like

Unlike dramatic ghost stories, bereavement encounters are often quiet and deeply personal. Many people report them in the weeks or months after a death, although some describe experiences years later.

Common forms include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Feeling the deceased standing or sitting nearby without clearly seeing them.
* Brief visual sightings, often in ordinary surroundings rather than supposedly haunted places.
* Hearing a familiar voice, footsteps or a characteristic phrase.
* Smelling a distinctive perfume, tobacco, flowers or another scent strongly associated with the deceased.
* Feeling a touch on the shoulder, a hand held, or the impression that someone has sat on the bed.
* Vivid dreams in which the deceased appears unusually lifelike and seems to communicate reassurance.

</div>

Researchers increasingly refer to these as **sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased**, recognising that many involve a powerful "felt presence" without a conventional visual hallucination. People often describe knowing someone is there without being able to explain exactly how they know.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Pure York]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 106 — Bereavement among elderly people: grief...</span></span></span>

An important feature is emotional tone. Although distressing experiences do occur, many bereaved individuals report calm, reassurance or a sense that the deceased is "all right". Rather than provoking fear, the experience may reduce loneliness or anxiety during intense grief. Studies of widowed spouses dating back to physician Dewi Rees's influential work found that such encounters were often regarded as helpful or emotionally neutral rather than pathological. Later research has broadly confirmed that these experiences are widespread across different bereaved populations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The phenomenology and impact of hallucinations concerning...by E Elsaesser · 2021 · Cited by 55 — In this study, we revisited Rees&#x27;s...</span></span></span>

## Why family meaning matters more than place legend

Traditional haunting stories are usually anchored to a location: an old manor, battlefield, abandoned hospital or reputedly haunted road. Bereavement encounters follow a different pattern.

The setting is often entirely ordinary. People describe sensing a deceased spouse while preparing breakfast, hearing a parent's voice in the family home, or briefly seeing a loved one in a familiar street. The significance comes not from where the event occurred but from who was experienced.

Because of this, these reports often remain private. Families may discuss them among themselves without ever presenting them as evidence of a haunted building or supernatural hotspot. A single encounter can become part of shared family history, reinforcing memories of the deceased rather than creating a local legend.

Anthropological research also shows that many cultures interpret continuing experiences of the dead as normal aspects of mourning rather than extraordinary paranormal events. Cross-cultural comparisons suggest that people may describe remarkably similar experiences while explaining them through very different religious or cultural frameworks. Some regard them as genuine visits, others as ancestors maintaining relationships, while still others see them as expected features of grief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33043856/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cultural research on post-bereavement perception or...by P Sabucedo · 2023 · Cited by 41 — Experiencing the continued presence of...</span></span></span>

This difference is significant for Fortean analysis. A haunted-house investigation asks whether multiple witnesses report recurring events at the same place. Bereavement reports instead focus on the continuing relationship between two people, making family context more informative than architectural history.

## Why psychologists no longer automatically treat these experiences as illness

During much of the twentieth century, seeing or sensing someone who had died was frequently described simply as a bereavement hallucination. While that term is still used in clinical literature, many researchers now argue that it can be misleading because it implies pathology where none may exist.

Modern bereavement research has found several consistent observations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Such experiences are relatively common among otherwise healthy people.
* They occur without other symptoms of psychosis in most cases.
* Many bereaved individuals do not disclose them because they fear ridicule.
* The experiences frequently provide comfort and help people adapt to loss.
* They are not, by themselves, considered evidence of mental illness.

</div>

This shift reflects broader changes in grief theory. Instead of assuming that healthy mourning requires emotionally "letting go", many psychologists now discuss **continuing bonds**—the idea that maintaining an inner relationship with the deceased can be a normal part of adaptation. Bereavement encounters may represent one expression of that continuing relationship rather than a failure to accept death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07481187.2023.2223593" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineThe impact of continuing bonds following bereavementby H Hewson · 2024 · Cited by 127 — Following bereavement, con...</span></span></span>

At the same time, clinicians distinguish ordinary grief experiences from situations where persistent hallucinations occur alongside severe confusion, dangerous behaviour or other psychiatric symptoms that require medical assessment.

## Why they remain important in Fortean research

Psychical researchers became interested in bereavement experiences long before modern [psychology]({{ 'psychology/' | relative_url }}) began studying grief systematically. Nineteenth-century investigators collected reports of [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}) appearing around the time of death, often calling them crisis apparitions when the experience coincided with an unexpected death or serious danger.

From a survival-of-consciousness perspective, these cases are potentially significant because some reports involve witnesses who claim they learned of a death only afterwards. Researchers have argued that apparently veridical details deserve careful documentation rather than immediate [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}).

Sceptical investigators, however, point to several difficulties:

* Memories are often recorded long after the event.
* Families naturally reinterpret ambiguous experiences after learning someone has died.
* [Coincidences]({{ 'coincidences/' | relative_url }}) become memorable while countless non-matching impressions are forgotten.
* Independent corroboration is frequently absent.

As a result, bereavement encounters occupy an enduring middle ground within Fortean literature. They are treated neither as simple [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}) nor as decisive proof of survival after death. Instead, they illustrate how extraordinary personal testimony can remain psychologically meaningful while resisting straightforward verification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/phenomena-relating-danger-death-bereavement/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2UVA School of Medicine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaPhenomena Related to Danger, Death, and BereavementJan 31, 2015 — Crisis impressions are marked by the abrupt awareness t...</span></span></span>

## How common are these reports?

Survey evidence suggests that experiences involving deceased relatives are far from rare.

In a 2023 survey by the Pew Research Center, 46% of American adults reported having been visited by a deceased family member in a dream, while 31% reported some other form of encounter outside dreams. Overall, more than half reported at least one type of interaction with a deceased relative during their lifetime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/08/23/many-americans-report-interacting-with-dead-relatives-in-dreams-or-other-ways/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pewresearch.org">[Pew Research Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pewresearch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pew Research Center Interacting with dead relatives: What Americans say they</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pew Research CenterInteracting with dead relatives: What Americans say they...August 23, 2023 — 23 Aug 2023 — Overall, 46% of Americans...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 23, 2023</span></span></span>

Clinical studies focusing specifically on widowed spouses have often reported similarly high rates of sensed presence or related experiences, although estimates vary depending on how researchers define the phenomenon and the population studied. Reviews covering psychology, psychiatry and anthropology consistently conclude that these experiences occur across cultures and are substantially more common than many people assume.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/schizophreniabulletin/article/46/6/1367/5939754" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicSensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 104 — Bereaved people often report h...</span></span></span>

These figures do not establish whether the experiences are paranormal. They do demonstrate that bereavement encounters are a widespread aspect of human grieving rather than an isolated curiosity.

## How to handle testimony without dismissing grief

Bereavement ghost reports present a challenge because two different questions are often confused.

The first asks whether the witness genuinely had the experience. In most cases there is little reason to [doubt]({{ 'doubt/' | relative_url }}) that they did. Research consistently finds that people describe these encounters as vivid, emotionally significant and often life-changing.

The second asks what caused the experience. Here, the evidence is much less decisive. Psychology offers explanations involving perception, memory, attachment and grief. Psychical researchers continue to examine whether some cases contain features that exceed conventional explanations. Neither position removes the emotional importance of the event for the person involved.

A balanced approach therefore avoids two extremes. It neither dismisses bereaved witnesses as irrational nor assumes that every sensed presence demonstrates survival after death. Instead, it recognises that grief can produce experiences that are both psychologically real and deeply meaningful, while remaining open to careful investigation where unusually strong evidence exists.

Within the wider study of ghost reports, bereavement encounters stand apart because they reveal that many of the most enduring ghost stories begin not with a haunted place, but with the continuing bond between people separated by death.
