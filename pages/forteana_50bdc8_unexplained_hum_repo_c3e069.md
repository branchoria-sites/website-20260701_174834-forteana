## Introduction

A mysterious local hum is Fortean not because it proves anything paranormal, but because it sits in the awkward space between real distress and uncertain evidence. People report a low, pulsing drone like a distant diesel engine, often strongest indoors at night, yet neighbours in the same street may hear nothing and standard noise checks may find no single source. That combination turns sound into a community mystery: residents compare notes, suspect factories or infrastructure, call councils, form [support groups]({{ 'support-groups/' | relative_url }}), and sometimes feel dismissed when investigators cannot confirm what they hear.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_unexplained_hum_repo_c3e069-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Odd Hums" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
The best evidence suggests there is no single “Hum”. Some cases are traceable to industrial machinery, ventilation, compressors, power equipment or other low-frequency sources. Others may involve tinnitus-like low-frequency sound perception, unusual hearing sensitivity, room resonance, stress, sleep disruption, or a mixture of all of these. This is why hum reports are valuable Fortean case studies: they show how place, health, technology and rumour can merge into a persistent anomaly without requiring a supernatural explanation.

## What Hum Witnesses Report

The classic hum report is strikingly consistent. Witnesses describe a deep, steady, intrusive sound: a rumble, throb, vibration, distant lorry, idling engine, or low electrical drone. It is often reported as worse at night, worse indoors, and difficult to locate by walking towards it. The World Hum Database describes the typical report as sounding like a car or truck engine idling outside or down the road, usually louder at night and indoors than outdoors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehum.info/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehum.info">[The World Hum Map]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehum.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The World Hum MapThe World Hum Map and Database Project - Dr Glen...We are tracking down the cause of worldwide anomolous low-frequency...</span></span></span>

That pattern matters because it partly explains why witnesses become anxious. A high-pitched alarm or obvious mechanical noise can usually be followed to a source. A low-frequency hum is different. Long wavelengths can pass through walls, reflect, form standing waves in rooms, and seem to come from everywhere or nowhere. The Institute of Acoustics notes that low-frequency noise can be generated by pumps, large fans, compressors, diesel engines, rotary machinery and power transmission systems, but that not everyone can hear it and, in many cases, no source is identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ioa.org.uk/briefing-notes/low-frequency-noise-annoyance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ioa.org.uk">[Institute of Acoustics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ioa.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of AcousticsLow Frequency Noise AnnoyanceLow Frequency Noise (LFN) can be an environmental noise problem. Hum witnesses also often report bodily and emotional effects: poor sleep, headaches, irritability, pressure sensations, dizziness, fatigue, and a feeling that the home has become unsafe or unpeaceful. These symptoms do not by themselves prove an external source; they do show why the issue becomes urgent. The World Health Organization’s environmental noise guidance links excessive noise with annoyance, sleep disturbance, tinnitus, hypertension and other health impacts, while its night-noise guidance treats noise-induced sleep disturbance as a health problem in its own right.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.who.int/tools/compendium-on-health-and-environment/environmental-noise" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: who.int">[World Health Organization]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">who.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Health OrganizationGuidance on environmental noiseExcessive noise can cause annoyance; in addition research shows it increases the...</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.polisnetwork.eu/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/who-night-noise-guidelines.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: polisnetwork.eu">[POLIS Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">polisnetwork.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">• While noise-induced sleep disturbance is viewed as a health problem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">POLIS NetworkNight noise guidelines for EuropeOctober 7, 2009 — by World Health Organization · 2009 · Cited by 659 — night noise exposure...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 7, 2009</span></span></span>

The Fortean element is the mismatch between testimony and verification. A small group may be adamant that something is happening, while officials, neighbours or acoustic instruments fail to capture a clear cause. That gap is where theories multiply: hidden industry, electrical grids, military systems, geological vibration, unusual atmospheric effects, tinnitus, or mass suggestion. The hum becomes less a single sound than a social problem organised around a sound.

## Why Famous Hum Cases Stay Unsettled

### Taos: the model modern mystery

The [Taos Hum]({{ 'taos-hum/' | relative_url }}) became one of the best-known modern cases in the early 1990s, when some residents of Taos, New Mexico, reported a persistent low-frequency noise usually compared to a diesel engine or tractor. Contemporary reporting stressed a feature that recurs in later cases: the noise was deeply disruptive for hearers, yet most residents did not hear it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-09-01-mn-30113-story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: latimes.com">[Los Angeles Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">latimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Los Angeles Times In Taos, Researchers Can Hum It, but They Can&#x27;t Name</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Los Angeles Times In Taos, Researchers Can Hum It, but They Can&#x27;t Name</span></span></span>

The case drew scientific attention because it was not merely a [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}) rumour. A 1995 account in *Echoes*, the newsletter of the Acoustical Society of America, described the Taos investigation as involving researchers from several institutions after the reports reached New Mexico’s congressional delegation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://acousticalsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/v5n3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: acousticalsociety.org">[Acoustical Society of America]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">acousticalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Acoustical Society of America The mystery of the Taos hum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acoustical Society of America The mystery of the Taos hum</span></span></span> Later summaries of United States hum studies note that the Taos work was inconclusive: investigators did not identify a known acoustic signal that could account for the reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icben.ethz.ch/2008/PDFs/Cowan.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icben.ethz.ch">[icben.ethz.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icben.ethz.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The results of hum studies in the United States</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The results of hum studies in the United States</span></span></span>

That inconclusive result is often misunderstood. It does not mean the witnesses invented the [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}). It means the investigation failed to connect the reports to a measurable, shared external source. For [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), Taos is important because it shows the structure of a durable anomaly: sincere reports, serious attention, plausible mundane hypotheses, and no decisive public closure.

### Kokomo: when a source is found but the story continues

[Kokomo]({{ 'kokomo/' | relative_url }}), Indiana, is useful because it complicates the idea that all hums are either “real” or “imagined”. In 2003, after many residents complained of a hum and associated health effects, an acoustic investigation identified two industrial low-frequency sources: a 36 Hz tone linked to a cooling tower at a DaimlerChrysler casting plant and a 10 Hz tone linked to an air compressor intake at Haynes International.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hum</span></span></span>

The important twist is that reports did not simply vanish once those sources were addressed. A later discussion by the lead investigator treated Kokomo alongside Taos and noted the difficulty of separating acoustical findings from non-acoustical effects attributed to the same event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://icben.ethz.ch/2008/PDFs/Cowan.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icben.ethz.ch">[icben.ethz.ch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icben.ethz.ch</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The results of hum studies in the United States</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The results of hum studies in the United States</span></span></span>

Kokomo therefore demonstrates a common risk in hum cases: investigators may find a real source, but that does not automatically explain every witness experience, every symptom, or every later report. Once a community has formed around a mystery, the social investigation can outlast the technical one.

### Windsor: the border-town hum and the problem of jurisdiction

The [Windsor]({{ 'windsor/' | relative_url }}) Hum, reported from Windsor, Ontario, from around 2011, became a major local anxiety because many suspected heavy industry across the Detroit River, especially the industrial area around Zug Island. The Canadian government funded a study by researchers from the University of Windsor and the University of Western Ontario after years of complaints about recurring vibration and noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2014/05/results-study-windsor-hum-released.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canada.ca.</span></span></span>

The 2014 summary found signals in the 30 Hz and 35 Hz bands associated with public reports, but it did not definitively pinpoint a source. Crucially, the report said the most probable bearing pointed south of Zug Island and that the bulk of observations did not support the simple claim that the source emanated from Zug Island. It listed possible sources such as quarry activity or industrial ventilation systems, while emphasising the complexity of acoustic propagation in local conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.international.gc.ca/department-ministere/windsor_hum_results-bourdonnement_windsor_resultats.aspx?lang=eng" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: international.gc.ca">[Global Affairs Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">international.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global Affairs Canada</span></span></span>

Windsor shows why hum mysteries can become political. A low-frequency sound may cross water, borders and jurisdictions. Residents want a responsible party; officials need evidence strong enough to act; suspected facilities may be outside the complaining authority’s control. The anxiety is not only “What is that sound?” but “Who has the power to make it stop?”

### Omagh and Lewis: recent cases show the pattern is still active

Recent UK and Irish cases show that hum reports have not faded into old paranormal books. In Omagh, County Tyrone, Fermanagh and Omagh District Council said its Environmental Health Service first received a complaint in August 2023, later received ten more, worked with a specialist noise consultant, and identified a particular industrial premises through a targeted screening exercise. But later monitoring did not produce conclusive evidence matching the earlier tone, and complainants subsequently said the hum was no longer audible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fermanaghomagh.com/article/statement-in-relation-to-investigation-into-complaint-regarding-a-humming-noise-in-omagh-town/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fermanaghomagh.com">[Fermanagh &amp; Omagh District Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fermanaghomagh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fermanaghomagh.com.</span></span></span>

The Isle of Lewis case in 2025 followed a familiar social script: residents reported a low-frequency hum, sleep disturbance and physical symptoms; a support group formed; possible sources were debated; and public discussion widened from local infrastructure to more speculative explanations. Reporting described more than 300 people joining a support group and noted disagreement over whether coping strategies such as relaxation or cognitive behavioural therapy should be framed as practical help or as dismissive advice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/government-adviser-mystery-hum-relax-lewis-zj9wx62dk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Islanders plagued by mystery hum told to live with it or get therapy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Times Islanders plagued by mystery hum told to live with it or get therapy</span></span></span>

These newer cases are important because they show the Hum as a living Fortean pattern, not a single historical curiosity. The same ingredients recur: night-time audibility, selective perception, local investigation, incomplete proof, health concern, online coordination and distrust of bland reassurance.

## Environmental and Bodily Causes

The most careful reading is pluralist: some hums are external sounds, some are internal perceptions, and some are probably interactions between both.

Low-frequency environmental noise is genuinely difficult. It is commonly defined as noise dominated by frequencies below about 200 Hz, and ordinary noise measures can understate its impact. Geoff Leventhall’s influential review in *Noise & Health* argues that conventional A-weighted measurements are often inadequate for low-frequency noise and that unresolved hum problems can produce learned aversion, annoyance and stress, especially when complainants receive unsympathetic treatment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15273024/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15273024/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Several features make low hums hard to investigate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **They travel well.** Low-frequency sound can carry over long distances and pass through building structures more easily than many higher-frequency sounds.
* **They are hard to localise.** Human hearing is worse at locating very low frequencies, so witnesses may misjudge direction.
* **They interact with rooms.** A bedroom, wall cavity or window can amplify a tone that is less obvious outdoors.
* **They are easily masked.** Daytime traffic, speech and ordinary activity can hide a hum that becomes obvious in night-time quiet.
* **They vary between listeners.** Hearing thresholds differ, so one person may be tormented while another hears nothing.

</div>

Because of these difficulties, the UK’s Salford University work for Defra developed a procedure to help environmental health practitioners handle low-frequency noise complaints. Its aim was not to produce a simple pass-or-fail nuisance rule, but to combine measurements, interviews and complainant logs so officials could judge whether an environmental sound was present and whether remedial action was possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26798517_Procedure_for_the_assessment_of_low-frequency_noise_complaints" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Procedure for the assessment of low-frequency</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) Procedure for the assessment of low-frequency</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loja.schiu.com/utilidades/artigos/UK-ProcedimentoRuidoBaixaFrequencia.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loja.schiu.com">[SCHIU]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loja.schiu.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Procedure for the assessment of low frequency noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Procedure for the assessment of low frequency noise</span></span></span>

The bodily side is just as important. A 2026 *PLOS ONE* study by Bastian Baumann and colleagues examined 28 people who reported unexplained low-frequency sound percepts. The researchers tested whether participants had unusually sensitive low-frequency hearing or whether they were hearing their own spontaneous otoacoustic emissions, tiny sounds produced by the inner ear. They found that, with a few exceptions, participants did not usually show unusually sensitive low-frequency thresholds, and the evidence did not support low-frequency otoacoustic emissions as the main explanation. The authors concluded that many cases may belong in the tinnitus family, especially where no external sound source can explain the percept.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0326818" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on plos.org.</span></span></span>

This does not make the Hum “not real”. Tinnitus is a real auditory experience, not a character flaw. The key distinction is whether the sound is an external acoustic event shared by the environment or a subjective sound generated within the auditory system. In practice, a sufferer may not care about that distinction at first; they want sleep, credibility and relief. But for investigation it matters enormously, because microphones can fix a machine but not a tinnitus-like percept.

## Why Communities Keep Investigating

Hum reports persist because they are not only sensory events. They become local trust problems.

A person who hears the hum first tries [ordinary explanations]({{ 'ordinary-causes/' | relative_url }}): the fridge, boiler, traffic, a neighbour’s pump, a nearby plant, an electrical substation. When those checks fail, the experience becomes stranger. If a partner or neighbour cannot hear it, the sufferer may feel isolated. If a council cannot measure it, they may feel dismissed. If a local industry is suspected but not proved responsible, frustration can become anger.

This is where local anxiety grows. The sound is often strongest in the place where people expect control: the bedroom. Sleep loss then magnifies attention, and attention magnifies the sound. Leventhall’s work on low-frequency noise notes the risk of learned aversion, where repeated exposure and distress make a sound increasingly intolerable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15273024/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> The result can be a loop: the more urgently someone listens for the hum, the harder it is to escape.

Community investigation can help. Shared logs, maps and recordings can reveal patterns that a single complaint cannot. Windsor’s study, for example, compared public reports with instrument data and found candidate frequency bands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.international.gc.ca/department-ministere/windsor_hum_results-bourdonnement_windsor_resultats.aspx?lang=eng" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: international.gc.ca">[Global Affairs Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">international.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global Affairs Canada</span></span></span> Omagh’s council investigation used specialist support and targeted screening rather than simply dismissing complainants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fermanaghomagh.com/article/statement-in-relation-to-investigation-into-complaint-regarding-a-humming-noise-in-omagh-town/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fermanaghomagh.com">[Fermanagh &amp; Omagh District Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fermanaghomagh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fermanaghomagh.com.</span></span></span>

But community investigation can also harden uncertainty into rumour. Once a suspected source is named informally, every vibration may be interpreted through that suspicion. If officials refuse to name a possible premises for legal reasons, as in Omagh, secrecy can look like concealment even when it is simply evidential caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/omagh-hum-sound-source-solved-lhdwj62gp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Omagh hum mystery solved</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Times Omagh hum mystery solved</span></span></span> If the suspected source is industrial, military or cross-border, ordinary noise annoyance can acquire the emotional force of environmental injustice.

The healthiest investigative stance is therefore double-sided. Take reports seriously, because low-frequency noise and tinnitus-like percepts can be deeply distressing. Avoid premature certainty, because hum cases often contain mixed evidence, multiple sources, and genuine limits of measurement.

## What Makes Hums Fortean Rather Than Merely Noisy

A hum becomes Fortean when it develops the shape of an anomaly: repeated testimony, partial patterns, failed closure, and explanations that are plausible but incomplete. Unlike a ghost story, a hum does not need a haunted house or a dramatic apparition. Its strangeness comes from persistence and ambiguity.

The strongest hum cases resist tidy storytelling in three ways.

First, **they blur external and internal reality**. A compressor, fan or cooling tower may produce a measurable tone, but a witness’s perception may also be shaped by hearing thresholds, tinnitus, stress, attention and the acoustics of a particular room. The same report can involve both environment and body.

Second, **they turn place into evidence**. Taos, Kokomo, Windsor, Omagh and Lewis are not interchangeable backdrops. Each case draws meaning from local geography, industry, politics and expectations of quiet. A rural island hum feels different from an industrial border-city hum, even if the acoustic description is similar.

Third, **they expose the limits of official reassurance**. “We found no source” is not the same as “nothing happened”. “It may be tinnitus” is not the same as “you are imagining it”. “A probable source was identified” is not the same as “the case is solved”. Hum reports survive in those gaps between technical language and lived experience.

That is why unexplained hums remain a distinctive branch of Forteana. They are not best understood as proof of hidden machines, secret weapons or paranormal forces. They are better understood as mysteries of perception under modern conditions: the low drone of infrastructure, the sensitivity of the ear, the vulnerability of sleep, and the social unease that appears when people can hear something their community cannot yet explain.

      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }
    return '';
  }

    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;
