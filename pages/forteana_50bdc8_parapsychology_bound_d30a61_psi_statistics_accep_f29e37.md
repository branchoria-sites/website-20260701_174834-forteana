## Introduction

[Parapsychology]({{ 'parapsychology/' | relative_url }}) is unusual because many of its most debated experiments do produce results that are reported as statistically significant, yet those results have not led to broad scientific acceptance of psi. This apparent contradiction often confuses readers. A statistically significant result simply indicates that an observed pattern would be unlikely under a particular chance model if all the assumptions of the analysis are correct. It does not, by itself, establish that a proposed explanation—such as telepathy or precognition—is correct. In mainstream science, extraordinary claims require not only statistical evidence but also consistent independent replication, robust methodology, and convincing exclusion of [ordinary explanations]({{ 'ordinary-causes/' | relative_url }}). Those additional standards are where the debate over psi has remained unresolved. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7883320/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAnomalous perception in a Ganzfeld condition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by PE Tressoldi · 2021 · Cited by 9 — A second major meta-analysis on a set of &#x27;autoganzfeld&#x27; studies was performed by Bem &amp; Honorton...</span></span></span>

## What above-chance scoring can and cannot show

Many laboratory psi studies use a simple statistical question: did participants score above the level expected by chance? In a typical ganzfeld telepathy experiment, for example, a participant choosing correctly 32% of the time when chance predicts 25% may produce a statistically significant result. Meta-analyses combining many such studies have sometimes reported small but positive average effects, leading some parapsychologists to argue that psi is detectable only through accumulated statistics rather than dramatic individual demonstrations. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7883320/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAnomalous perception in a Ganzfeld condition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by PE Tressoldi · 2021 · Cited by 9 — A second major meta-analysis on a set of &#x27;autoganzfeld&#x27; studies was performed by Bem &amp; Honorton...</span></span></span>

However, statistical significance answers only one question: whether the observed data are unlikely under a specified null hypothesis. It does not answer several other questions that are crucial for scientific acceptance:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Whether the experimental controls ruled out every plausible conventional explanation.
* Whether the effect appears consistently across independent laboratories.
* Whether the estimated effect size is stable rather than fluctuating with methodology.
* Whether the statistical model itself reflects how the data were actually collected and analysed.

</div>

A small but statistically significant deviation from chance can therefore represent several possibilities. It could indicate a genuine unknown phenomenon, a subtle methodological weakness, selective reporting, underestimated sources of bias, or simply an overestimate produced by random variation. Statistics alone cannot distinguish among these alternatives. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aiprinc.org/documents/para-ac02_Hyman_2010_Meta_Analysis.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aiprinc.org">[aiprinc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aiprinc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1985). Meta-analysis of psi ganzfeld research: A response to. Hyman. Journal of Parapsychology, 49, 51–91. Hyman, R. (1957). Review of M...</span></span></span>

This distinction explains why supporters and critics often agree on the numerical calculations while disagreeing sharply about what those calculations mean.

## Publication bias and flexible analysis

One reason mainstream researchers remain cautious is that small statistical effects are especially vulnerable to biases that can accumulate across many experiments.

Publication bias—sometimes called the "file drawer problem"—occurs when positive findings are more likely to be published than negative ones. If unsuccessful psi experiments remain unpublished, published literature may overstate the apparent strength of an effect. This problem affects many scientific disciplines and is not unique to parapsychology, but it becomes particularly important when expected effects are already very small. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication_bias" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Publication bias</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Publication bias</span></span></span>

Flexible analysis presents another challenge. Researchers may unintentionally make numerous analytical decisions after viewing the data, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* choosing among several outcome measures;
* excluding particular observations;
* stopping data collection after apparently promising results;
* testing multiple statistical models before reporting one.

</div>

Each individual decision may seem reasonable, but together they increase the chance of finding apparently significant results that are difficult to reproduce later. Modern metascience recognises these practices as a general reproducibility problem across [psychology]({{ 'psychology/' | relative_url }}) and other behavioural sciences rather than something exclusive to psi research. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.00556" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The history of ganzfeld research illustrates these concerns. During the 1980s, psychologist Ray Hyman argued that many published studies suffered from methodological weaknesses, including optional stopping, inadequate randomisation, incomplete documentation and possible sensory leakage. Charles Honorton disputed many of those conclusions while agreeing that stronger protocols were needed. ## Why predictable replication remains the key test

The central issue separating statistical significance from accepted scientific evidence is replication.

Science rarely treats a single significant experiment as decisive. Confidence grows when different research groups, working independently and using rigorous protocols, obtain similar results under conditions that minimise opportunities for bias.

In psi research, this has proved difficult. Successive meta-analyses have reached different conclusions depending on which studies were included, how methodological quality was evaluated and which statistical models were applied. Some reviews report a small but persistent above-chance effect, whereas critics argue that the apparent signal weakens when higher-quality studies and unsuccessful replications receive greater weight. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7883320/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2aiprinc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAnomalous perception in a Ganzfeld condition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by PE Tressoldi · 2021 · Cited by 9 — A second major meta-analysis on a set of &#x27;autoganzfeld&#x27; studies was performed by Bem &amp; Honorton...</span></span></span>

The debate therefore shifted from asking whether isolated experiments reach conventional significance thresholds to asking whether researchers can reliably predict when psi effects will occur. Mainstream science generally expects a genuine phenomenon to produce dependable results under clearly specified conditions. If an effect appears only intermittently or varies substantially between laboratories without a well-supported explanatory theory, confidence remains limited.

This is especially important because a statistically significant effect can be extremely small. An effect size may be mathematically distinguishable from chance while still being too inconsistent for practical prediction. From the perspective of scientific acceptance, reproducibility matters more than the existence of isolated positive findings.

## Why meta-analysis has not settled the debate

Parapsychology has relied heavily on meta-analysis because individual experiments often lack sufficient statistical power to detect very small effects. Combining many studies can increase sensitivity and reveal patterns that are difficult to see in single experiments.

Supporters argue that repeated small effects appearing across decades deserve serious consideration. Critics respond that meta-analysis cannot compensate for weaknesses in the underlying studies. If the original literature contains publication bias, inconsistent methodology or questionable analytical choices, combining those studies may simply produce a more precise estimate of a biased result rather than stronger evidence for psi. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aiprinc.org/documents/para-ac02_Hyman_2010_Meta_Analysis.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aiprinc.org">[aiprinc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aiprinc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1985). The disagreement is therefore less about whether the statistical calculations are correct than about what assumptions should be made regarding study quality, missing data and replication failures.

## Why the distinction matters within Forteana

Within the wider world of [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), unusual reports often remain valuable as intriguing anomalies regardless of whether they receive scientific confirmation. Parapsychology adopts a narrower goal. It attempts to transform anecdotal or experimental anomalies into evidence for a real, measurable phenomenon.

The distinction between statistical significance and accepted evidence marks the boundary between those approaches. Above-chance results can justify further investigation, motivate improved experiments and suggest hypotheses worth testing. They do not, by themselves, establish that psi exists as an accepted scientific phenomenon.

For that transition to occur, mainstream science generally expects a convergence of evidence: rigorous experimental controls, transparent analysis, successful independent replication, stable effect sizes and plausible exclusion of conventional explanations.
