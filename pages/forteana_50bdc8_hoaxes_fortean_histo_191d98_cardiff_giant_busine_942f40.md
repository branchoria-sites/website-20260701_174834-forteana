## Introduction

The Cardiff Giant became one of the most profitable [hoaxes]({{ 'hoaxes/' | relative_url }}) in nineteenth-century America not because everyone accepted it as genuine, but because people could not agree on what they were looking at. Presented in 1869 as a newly unearthed “petrified man”, the ten-foot figure sat at the intersection of biblical belief, emerging scientific archaeology, commercial entertainment and sensational journalism. Visitors paid not simply to see a giant, but to participate in an argument over whether it represented ancient history, religious proof, Native American art or outright fraud. That uncertainty proved commercially valuable. Even after experts declared it a fake, the controversy itself kept audiences coming, culminating in an extraordinary spectacle where showman P. T. Barnum exhibited his own counterfeit version of an already counterfeit object. The Cardiff Giant therefore illustrates an important pattern in Fortean history: disputed evidence can become more profitable than settled truth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/cardiff-giant-was-just-big-hoax-180965274/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine The Cardiff Giant Was Just a Big Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian MagazineThe Cardiff Giant Was Just a Big HoaxOctober 16, 2017 — 16 Oct 2017 — The story, which began on this day in 1869, was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2017</span></span></span>

## Why a petrified giant fit its moment

The Cardiff Giant emerged during a period when Americans were debating geology, evolution and the literal interpretation of the Bible. Fossils were becoming familiar to the public, yet scientific standards for archaeology and palaeontology were still developing. Reports of unusual discoveries circulated widely through [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}), and many readers were prepared to entertain the possibility that remarkable physical evidence might suddenly rewrite history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/cardiff-giant-was-just-big-hoax-180965274/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine The Cardiff Giant Was Just a Big Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian MagazineThe Cardiff Giant Was Just a Big HoaxOctober 16, 2017 — 16 Oct 2017 — The story, which began on this day in 1869, was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 16, 2017</span></span></span>

George Hull, the tobacconist who organised the deception, later explained that the project grew partly from arguments over the biblical passage in Genesis referring to giants. According to contemporary accounts, Hull wanted both to expose what he regarded as religious credulity and to profit from doing so. He commissioned a gypsum block to be carved into a weathered human figure, artificially aged it with acids and abrasion, and secretly buried it on the farm of his cousin William "Stub" Newell before arranging its dramatic "discovery" nearly a year later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Giant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cardiff Giant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cardiff Giant</span></span></span>

Importantly, the giant was not convincing because it perfectly resembled an ancient human body. Even many early observers noticed anatomical oddities and visible tool marks. Instead, it occupied a plausible middle ground between certainty and mystery. Some visitors interpreted it as a petrified human, others as an ancient statue, while sceptics dismissed it as a recent carving. That ambiguity created discussion rather than immediate [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}), allowing competing interpretations to coexist long enough for commercial exploitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/509948" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">768. ## How exhibition money kept the story alive

The financial success of the Cardiff Giant depended on maintaining uncertainty rather than resolving it. Newell quickly erected a tent over the excavation site and initially charged admission before raising the price as crowds increased. Hundreds of visitors arrived daily, and local hotels, restaurants and transport providers also benefited from the sudden influx of tourists. The discovery became a regional economic event as much as a scientific curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Giant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cardiff Giant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cardiff Giant</span></span></span>

Once the initial excitement peaked, ownership shifted from the original conspirators to investors led by David Hannum, who recognised that the giant's value lay in touring exhibitions. Instead of quietly exposing the fraud or withdrawing the object, the syndicate moved it to larger cities where fresh audiences could continue debating its authenticity. Every newspaper report questioning the giant also served as advertising, encouraging readers to judge the evidence for themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Giant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cardiff Giant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cardiff Giant</span></span></span>

Scientific criticism did not immediately destroy the attraction. Yale palaeontologist Othniel Charles Marsh pointed out that the statue consisted of soluble gypsum, retained fresh carving marks and could not plausibly have survived centuries underground. Other investigators questioned why a well had been dug at precisely that location. Yet these expert opinions became part of the entertainment rather than an immediate ending. Visitors could compare expert testimony with their own impressions, turning scepticism into another reason to buy a ticket.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/the-cardiff-giant-fools-the-nation-145-years-ago" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the cardiff giant fools the nation 145 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cardiff Giant Fools the Nation, 145 Years Ago16 Oct 2014 — “It is rather rich,” quipped the Philadelphia Inquirer, “that we sh...</span></span></span>

This dynamic distinguishes the Cardiff Giant from a simple confidence trick. Instead of disappearing once challenged, it entered a prolonged public debate in which competing authorities—scientists, clergy, journalists, entrepreneurs and spectators—each claimed the right to interpret the evidence. The exhibition profited from the continued existence of disagreement.

## What Barnum's rival fake reveals about spectacle

The most revealing episode came after P. T. Barnum attempted to purchase the giant for exhibition. When the owners refused his offer, Barnum commissioned an almost identical copy and advertised it as the genuine Cardiff Giant, claiming that the original exhibition displayed the fake. Rather than undermining public interest, this created a second layer of deception. Audiences could now choose between two competing giants, each accusing the other of fraud.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/the-cardiff-giant-fools-the-nation-145-years-ago" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the cardiff giant fools the nation 145 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cardiff Giant Fools the Nation, 145 Years Ago16 Oct 2014 — “It is rather rich,” quipped the Philadelphia Inquirer, “that we sh...</span></span></span>

This "fake of a fake" transformed authenticity into a theatrical performance. Barnum's advertisements asked provocative questions rather than providing answers, encouraging visitors to witness the controversy firsthand. Reports suggest that Barnum's version sometimes attracted larger crowds than the original because the rivalry itself had become the attraction. Newspapers recognised the absurdity of a fraud competing against another fraud, yet that very absurdity generated additional publicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/the-cardiff-giant-fools-the-nation-145-years-ago" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[HISTORY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the cardiff giant fools the nation 145 years ago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cardiff Giant Fools the Nation, 145 Years Ago16 Oct 2014 — “It is rather rich,” quipped the Philadelphia Inquirer, “that we sh...</span></span></span>

The legal dispute that followed further illustrates how commercial spectacle overtook factual resolution. Hannum sued Barnum for claiming the original giant was fake, but the case collapsed into irony because both exhibits were fraudulent. The courts ultimately refused to punish someone for describing a fake object as fake. Meanwhile, public attention remained focused on the competing exhibitions rather than on establishing historical truth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Giant" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cardiff Giant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cardiff Giant</span></span></span>

The episode is also linked—probably incorrectly—to the famous saying, "There's a sucker born every minute." Contemporary evidence suggests the remark was associated with David Hannum rather than Barnum, while later [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}) shifted the quotation to the better-known showman. ## Why the Cardiff Giant remains significant in Fortean history

Within the history of Fortean claims, the Cardiff Giant demonstrates that contested evidence can acquire a cultural life independent of belief. The hoax succeeded because it resonated with existing debates about scripture, ancient peoples and scientific authority, but it endured because no single institution could immediately close the argument for the wider public. The attraction monetised uncertainty itself.

Later Fortean hoaxes would repeatedly employ the same strategy. Rather than demanding unquestioning acceptance, they encouraged audiences to occupy an intermediate position in which possibility seemed more exciting than certainty.
