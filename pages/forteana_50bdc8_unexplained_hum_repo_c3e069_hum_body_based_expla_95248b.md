## Introduction

One of the most important explanations for unexplained hum reports is that, in some cases, the sound originates within the listener rather than the environment. This does not mean the [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}) is imaginary. Tinnitus, unusual sensitivity to low-frequency sound, altered auditory processing, stress, and sleep disruption can all produce a genuine perception of humming, droning, or vibration without a shared external source. These possibilities help explain a long-standing feature of hum cases: one person may be profoundly disturbed while family members, neighbours, and acoustic instruments detect nothing unusual. Research suggests that body-based mechanisms probably account for some reports, while other cases involve genuine environmental low-frequency noise. The challenge is distinguishing between the two rather than assuming that every report has the same cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2686891/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2BMJ Best Practice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tinnitus: Characteristics, Causes, Mechanisms, and Treatmentsby BI Han · 2009 · Cited by 515 — Tinnitus is defined as a phantom audito...</span></span></span>

## Can a Hum Come From the Listener?

The best-established internal explanation is subjective tinnitus: the perception of sound without an external acoustic source. Although tinnitus is popularly associated with ringing, clinicians recognise many other descriptions, including buzzing, hissing, roaring and humming. Some patients report sounds with an unusually low pitch that resemble an idling engine or distant machinery, making them easy to mistake for environmental noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bestpractice.bmj.com/topics/en-gb/364" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bestpractice.bmj.com">[BMJ Best Practice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bestpractice.bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BMJ Best PracticeTinnitus - Symptoms, diagnosis and treatmentSounds may be soft or loud and may be described as ringing, humming, buzzing...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters in Fortean discussions because a low-frequency internal sound can initially behave exactly like an external mystery. A person may search the house, inspect electrical appliances, or go outside expecting to find a mechanical source. Only after repeated failures—or after noticing the sound follows them between locations—does an internal explanation become plausible.

Recent research focusing specifically on people who report "the Hum" has strengthened this possibility. Investigators tested individuals who regularly perceived unexplained low-frequency sounds and found little evidence that unusually sensitive low-frequency hearing or audible sounds produced by the ear itself explained most cases. Instead, the findings pointed towards low-frequency subjective tinnitus as the most likely mechanism for many participants, while still acknowledging that genuine external hums also exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.popularmechanics.com/science/health/a71629139/the-hum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: popularmechanics.com">[Popular Mechanics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">popularmechanics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Popular Mechanics A Mysterious Hum Has Plagued the Earth for 50 Years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientists May Have Finally Found Its Source.June 25, 2026 — For over 50 years, a mysterious low-frequency noise known as &quot;the Hum&quot; has b...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2026</span></span></span>

### Low-frequency tinnitus and unusual sensitivity

Low-frequency tinnitus appears to be less common than the high-pitched form familiar to most clinicians, but it can be especially confusing because its character resembles ordinary environmental sounds. Instead of a whistle or ring, sufferers may describe:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* A distant diesel engine.
* An electrical transformer.
* Heavy ventilation equipment.
* A continuous drone.
* A deep vibration or rumble.

</div>

Because humans are relatively poor at locating low-frequency sounds, whether internal or external, sufferers may genuinely feel that the sound comes from "everywhere" rather than from one ear or one direction. This perceptual ambiguity makes self-diagnosis difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bestpractice.bmj.com/topics/en-gb/364" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bestpractice.bmj.com">[BMJ Best Practice+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bestpractice.bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BMJ Best PracticeTinnitus - Symptoms, diagnosis and treatmentSounds may be soft or loud and may be described as ringing, humming, buzzing...</span></span></span>

Research into complaints about low-frequency noise has also found that some complainants are exposed to measurable environmental sound while others appear to experience low-frequency tinnitus instead. The two groups may describe remarkably similar symptoms despite having different underlying causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vbn.aau.dk/files/54563946/LF_27_1_1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vbn.aau.dk">[Aalborg Universitets forskningsportal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vbn.aau.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aalborg Universitets forskningsportalA detailed study of low-frequency noise complaintsby CS Pedersen — The results showed that some of t...</span></span></span>

## Stress, Sleep Loss, and Symptom Loops

One reason hum reports can become persistent is that hearing and attention form a feedback loop. Once an intrusive sound becomes emotionally important, the brain allocates more attention to detecting it. Increased monitoring makes the sound seem more noticeable, which raises distress, which further increases monitoring.

This cycle is well recognised in tinnitus research. Psychological stress does not simply arise because tinnitus is unpleasant; stress can also amplify the perceived loudness and intrusiveness of tinnitus through interactions between auditory processing, attention, and emotional centres of the brain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188965/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The association between stress, emotional states, and tinnitusby JD Patil · 2023 · Cited by 92 — Patients suffering from tinnitus perc...</span></span></span>

Sleep disruption can intensify the same process. Many hum reports describe symptoms becoming worst late at night, when external background noise falls away and bedrooms become unusually quiet. In that environment, internal auditory sensations receive less masking from ordinary environmental sound and therefore become more noticeable. Poor sleep then increases fatigue, anxiety and sensory vigilance the following day, creating another reinforcing cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10188965/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The association between stress, emotional states, and tinnitusby JD Patil · 2023 · Cited by 92 — Patients suffering from tinnitus perc...</span></span></span>

This feedback model helps explain why some long-running hum cases become increasingly distressing even when no environmental change can be identified.

## Why Internal Causes Do Not Mean Fake Reports

A common misunderstanding is that if a hum has an internal origin then the witness must be mistaken or inventing the experience. Modern hearing science does not support that conclusion.

Subjective tinnitus is a genuine sensory perception generated within the auditory system. The sound is real to the person experiencing it despite lacking an external acoustic source. Brain imaging and neurophysiological research indicate that tinnitus reflects altered neural activity across auditory pathways and interconnected brain networks rather than conscious imagination or deliberate fabrication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2686891/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tinnitus: Characteristics, Causes, Mechanisms, and Treatmentsby BI Han · 2009 · Cited by 515 — Tinnitus is defined as a phantom audito...</span></span></span>

This distinction is important when evaluating unexplained hum reports:

* **The experience is genuine.** The listener is perceiving a sound.
* **The source may differ.** The sound may arise from internal auditory processing rather than the outside world.
* **Environmental investigations may still be worthwhile.** Internal explanations cannot automatically dismiss reports because some communities have experienced measurable low-frequency industrial noise.
* **Different people may experience different mechanisms.** One household member may hear external low-frequency noise while another develops heightened awareness or tinnitus that continues after the original trigger has disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vbn.aau.dk/files/54563946/LF_27_1_1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vbn.aau.dk">[Aalborg Universitets forskningsportal+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vbn.aau.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aalborg Universitets forskningsportalA detailed study of low-frequency noise complaintsby CS Pedersen — The results showed that some of t...</span></span></span>

For investigators of Fortean reports, this means [sincerity]({{ 'sincerity/' | relative_url }}) and external reality are separate questions. Witnesses can honestly report what they hear even if the ultimate source lies within the auditory system.

## Why Body-Based Explanations Fit Some Hum Cases Better Than Others

Certain recurring features make an internal explanation more plausible, although none is individually decisive.

A body-based origin becomes more likely when the hum:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* persists across many different locations,
* remains audible when no measurable environmental source can be identified,
* becomes strongest in quiet settings,
* varies with stress, fatigue or sleep quality,
* accompanies other tinnitus symptoms or hearing changes.

</div>

Conversely, reports become harder to explain solely by tinnitus when multiple unrelated listeners independently hear the same sound, when investigators record matching low-frequency signals, or when removing an identified mechanical source reduces complaints. Such cases suggest an environmental contribution, even if individual sensitivity determines who notices it most strongly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vbn.aau.dk/files/54563946/LF_27_1_1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vbn.aau.dk">[Aalborg Universitets forskningsportal+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vbn.aau.dk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aalborg Universitets forskningsportalA detailed study of low-frequency noise complaintsby CS Pedersen — The results showed that some of t...</span></span></span>

Rather than treating "the Hum" as either entirely external or entirely psychological, current evidence supports a mixed picture. Some famous cases appear linked to identifiable low-frequency noise, others are consistent with tinnitus-like perception, and many probably involve interactions between environmental sound, individual hearing, attention, stress and expectation. That combination explains why unexplained hum reports remain a compelling Fortean subject without requiring a single universal explanation.
