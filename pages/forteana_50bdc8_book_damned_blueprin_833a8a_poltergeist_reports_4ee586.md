## Introduction

Poltergeist-like reports occupy a smaller place in [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})'s archive than his famous collections of strange falls, mysterious lights and unexplained objects, but they are important because they shifted [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}) into the domestic sphere. Instead of looking upward at unusual events in the sky, Fort also examined reports of houses where stones appeared to fly from invisible sources, windows shattered without an identified culprit, or household objects behaved in ways that witnesses and police could not readily explain. He did not present these cases as proof of spirits. Rather, he treated them as examples of reports that remained awkwardly unresolved after official investigation, making them another class of his "damned" facts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/fort/damn/damn13.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">London Times,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Sacred Text ArchiveThe Book of the Damned: 13July 1, 1919 — Instances of stones that have been thrown, or that have fallen, upon...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 1919</span></span></span>

Within *[The Book]({{ 'the-book/' | relative_url }}) of the Damned*, these accounts broadened the Fortean project by showing that anomalies were not confined to remote landscapes or spectacular natural phenomena. They could unfold in ordinary homes, involve multiple witnesses and attract direct attention from neighbours, police and [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}). That shift helped establish a lasting Fortean interest in everyday experiences that resist straightforward classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newberry.org/blog/poltergeists-the-paranormal-and-flying-discs-in-the-sky" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newberry.org">[Newberry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newberry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">poltergeists the paranormal and flying discs in the sky</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Newberry LibraryPoltergeists, the Paranormal, and Flying Discs in the Sky8 Oct 2024 — The Book of the Damned is organized into twenty-eig...</span></span></span>

## Household disturbance reports

Fort introduced his discussion by acknowledging that his own collection merged into what were commonly labelled "poltergeist" cases. His interest centred less on ghost traditions than on recurring reports of physical disturbances that appeared to lack an identifiable cause. He drew heavily from newspaper accounts, especially British reporting, assembling incidents that shared a recognisable pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/fort/damn/damn13.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">London Times,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Sacred Text ArchiveThe Book of the Damned: 13July 1, 1919 — Instances of stones that have been thrown, or that have fallen, upon...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 1919</span></span></span>

Typical reports in his archive include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Houses repeatedly struck by stones or other small objects from directions that observers could not determine.
* Windows broken despite guards, neighbours or police watching the property.
* Localised attacks affecting one household rather than an entire neighbourhood.
* Episodes that continued despite organised attempts to identify pranksters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/fort/damn/damn13.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">London Times,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Sacred Text ArchiveThe Book of the Damned: 13July 1, 1919 — Instances of stones that have been thrown, or that have fallen, upon...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 1919</span></span></span>

</div>

One of Fort's better-known examples came from *The Times* of London, describing houses in Bermondsey in 1872 that were bombarded with stones over several hours. According to the contemporary report, windows were smashed, children injured and numerous policemen stationed nearby, yet no source for the missiles could be identified. Fort presented the account largely through quotation, allowing the apparent failure of conventional investigation to become part of the anomaly itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/fort/damn/damn13.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">London Times,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Sacred Text ArchiveThe Book of the Damned: 13July 1, 1919 — Instances of stones that have been thrown, or that have fallen, upon...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 1919</span></span></span>

Another case, also drawn from *The Times*, concerned Mrs Charton's house at Sutton Court House in Chiswick in 1841. Windows reportedly continued to break while police officers and members of the household maintained watch around a detached building enclosed by walls. Fort highlighted the repeated inability of investigators to catch anyone responsible, not because he claimed supernatural forces had been demonstrated, but because the official record itself acknowledged an unresolved problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newberry.org/blog/poltergeists-the-paranormal-and-flying-discs-in-the-sky" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newberry.org">[Newberry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newberry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">poltergeists the paranormal and flying discs in the sky</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Newberry LibraryPoltergeists, the Paranormal, and Flying Discs in the Sky8 Oct 2024 — The Book of the Damned is organized into twenty-eig...</span></span></span>

These examples illustrate an important feature of Fort's method. The focus was not spectacular violence but persistent, apparently physical events recorded in ordinary settings and discussed in respectable newspapers.

## Documentation gaps and witness pressure

Fort's treatment of these incidents differed from later paranormal authors who attempted to identify a single hidden mechanism. Instead, he concentrated on the weaknesses and contradictions within the documentation itself.

Several features repeatedly attracted his attention:

* **Multiple witnesses without consensus.** Many reports involved families, neighbours and police, yet agreement about what had happened remained incomplete.
* **Institutional acknowledgement without resolution.** Authorities often investigated but reached no satisfying conclusion.
* **Fragmentary reporting.** Newspaper accounts frequently described dramatic beginnings but offered little follow-up explaining whether the mystery had eventually been solved.
* **Absence of decisive evidence.** Neither supernatural explanations nor ordinary ones could be demonstrated from the surviving reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/fort/damn/damn13.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">London Times,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Sacred Text ArchiveThe Book of the Damned: 13July 1, 1919 — Instances of stones that have been thrown, or that have fallen, upon...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 1919</span></span></span>

These gaps were central to Fort's argument. He was less interested in proving invisible agents than in showing how unresolved events accumulated within the historical record. Scientific and legal institutions generally required decisive explanations, while newspapers often lost interest once a story ceased to be newsworthy. The resulting archive contained many incomplete narratives that could not easily be classified as either established fact or exposed fraud.

This approach explains why Fort often reproduced lengthy extracts from newspapers instead of rewriting them into polished stories. The original wording, with its uncertainties, official statements and missing conclusions, demonstrated the untidy character of anomalous evidence more effectively than a simplified retelling could.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/bookofdamnedbych00fortrich" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveThe book of the damned, by Charles Fort25 Jan 2007 — The book of the damned, by Charles Fort. by: Fort, Charles, 1874-193...</span></span></span>

## Why uncanny events broadened Forteana

The inclusion of poltergeist-like reports expanded the scope of Forteana in several important ways.

First, they introduced a strongly witness-centred perspective. A rain of frogs or an unusual astronomical [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) might be recorded by specialists, but household disturbances were experienced by families, servants, neighbours and local police. The credibility of ordinary people therefore became part of the evidence itself.

Second, these reports blurred disciplinary boundaries. They touched criminal investigation, journalism, [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}) and emerging psychical research simultaneously. Fort did not attempt to resolve those competing interpretations. Instead, he placed them alongside meteorological anomalies, unexplained falls and archaeological oddities as different expressions of the same larger problem: the persistence of observations that resisted stable classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — Rains of blood and frogs, mysterious disappea...</span></span></span>

Finally, domestic disturbances reinforced Fort's broader challenge to intellectual certainty. A broken window was not extraordinary because broken windows never happen; it became anomalous only when repeated investigation failed to establish how or why the damage occurred. In this sense, the anomaly lay as much in the inadequacy of explanation as in the event itself.

## Fort's lasting influence on later Fortean thinking

Although *The Book of the Damned* devoted far more space to atmospheric and physical anomalies than to household disturbances, its treatment of poltergeist-like reports proved influential because it offered a template that later Fortean writers repeatedly adopted.

The pattern became familiar:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* collect numerous independently reported incidents;
* compare recurring features without insisting on a single explanation;
* preserve contradictions rather than smoothing them away;
* treat institutional uncertainty as evidence worth examining in its own right.

</div>

Later writers investigating hauntings, recurrent spontaneous disturbances and other domestic anomalies frequently inherited this documentary style, even when they disagreed about causes. Some pursued psychological interpretations, others parapsychological ones, while sceptics emphasised misperception or fraud.
