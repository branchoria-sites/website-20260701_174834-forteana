## Introduction

*The Book of the Damned* (1919) made [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }}) famous not simply because it collected reports of strange phenomena, but because it presented those reports as part of a sustained literary attack on intellectual certainty. Rather than arguing that every mysterious event was genuine, Fort used anomalies to expose what he saw as the overconfidence of institutions that claimed to possess complete explanations of reality. The book is therefore best understood as satire directed at certainty itself. Its catalogue of impossible rains, unexplained [disappearances]({{ 'disappearances/' | relative_url }}) and curious astronomical observations was inseparable from its mockery of scientific dogmatism, sweeping theories and the human desire to believe that every puzzle has already been solved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

This combination of anomaly collection and ironic criticism is what distinguished *The Book of the Damned* from earlier compilations of marvels. Fort did not simply ask readers to accept extraordinary claims. Instead, he encouraged them to become suspicious of anyone—scientist, philosopher or believer—who claimed final authority over knowledge. That scepticism extended even to his own ideas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

## Why the book felt different

Most earlier books about unexplained events attempted either to defend supernatural explanations or to dismiss unusual reports as [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}). Fort pursued a different strategy. He assembled hundreds of reports drawn from [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}), scientific journals and official publications, then arranged them into a running argument that the greatest mystery was not the events themselves but the confidence with which authorities declared them impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or...Read more...</span></span></span>

The famous opening declaration captures this approach. Fort announces "a procession of the damned", defining the damned as "the excluded"—observations left outside accepted systems of knowledge. The phrase reframes the issue from whether an individual report is true to how institutions decide what deserves investigation in the first place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472.epub.images" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Book of the DamnedTHE BOOK OF THE DAMNED. 1. A procession of the damned. By the damned, I mean the excluded. We shal...</span></span></span>

The result is a book that often resembles a courtroom cross-examination rather than a scientific treatise. Each curious report becomes another witness called to challenge the confidence of established explanations rather than a standalone proof of paranormal phenomena.

## Data lists and literary attack

Fort's method depended on accumulation. A single report of fish falling from the sky or an unexplained luminous object could be dismissed as error. Dozens of similar reports from different places and times forced readers to confront a more uncomfortable question: why were such accounts repeatedly published yet rarely incorporated into accepted knowledge?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or...Read more...</span></span></span>

The catalogue itself, however, was only half of the performance. Between examples Fort inserted sarcastic commentary, invented dialogues, exaggerated comparisons and deliberately provocative claims. He mocked the confidence of experts by imitating their language while stretching it towards absurdity. This rhetorical style made the book feel less like an encyclopaedia of mysteries than a satirical essay disguised as an archive.

Several recurring techniques define the book's humour:

* **Mock certainty.** Fort exaggerated scientific confidence until it sounded ridiculous, suggesting that official explanations often rested on convention as much as evidence.
* **Role reversal.** Scientists became the credulous believers, while collectors of anomalies became the sceptics asking awkward questions.
* **Accumulation as comedy.** Endless lists of strange reports create both intellectual pressure and comic excess, making readers aware of the difficulty of maintaining neat classifications.
* **Ironical exaggeration.** Fort frequently pushed arguments beyond plausibility to expose the weaknesses of rigid systems rather than to establish new doctrines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

Because of this style, modern readers sometimes mistake Fort's rhetoric for literal advocacy of every speculation he mentions. His humour often depends on blurring that boundary.

## Science as Fort's target

Fort's principal target was not scientific investigation itself but what he regarded as scientific absolutism. He repeatedly compared scientific orthodoxy with earlier religious certainty, arguing that dominant intellectual systems naturally exclude inconvenient observations in order to preserve coherence. According to Fort, every age constructs its own "dominant" worldview, and each dominant eventually dismisses facts that fail to fit its framework.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

Importantly, Fort criticised attitudes rather than specific disciplines alone. Geology, astronomy, evolution and physics appear throughout the book, but usually as examples of broader habits of thought: confidence, classification and the temptation to confuse useful models with complete truth. The satire therefore reaches beyond early twentieth-century science and addresses a recurring human tendency to mistake successful explanations for exhaustive ones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

This distinction explains why the book continues to attract both admirers and critics. Readers sympathetic to scientific inquiry can still recognise Fort's warning against institutional complacency without accepting his treatment of anomalous evidence.

## Why Fort undermined his own theories

One of the most distinctive features of *The Book of the Damned* is that Fort repeatedly weakens his own speculative explanations. He famously introduces imaginative possibilities—including the notion of a "[Super-Sargasso Sea]({{ 'super-sargasso/' | relative_url }})", a mysterious region where lost objects accumulate before falling back to Earth—but rarely insists they are literally true. Instead, such ideas function as demonstrations that if accepted theories can rely on assumptions, alternative assumptions can be invented just as easily.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_Damned" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Book of the Damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Book of the Damned</span></span></span>

This habit serves several purposes.

First, it prevents readers from replacing one rigid orthodoxy with another. If Fort offered a complete theory explaining every anomaly, he would become guilty of the same certainty he criticised.

Second, it shifts attention away from the speculative explanation and back towards the neglected [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}). The existence of unexplained reports matters more, in Fort's argument, than any immediate attempt to explain them.

Third, it turns the entire book into a satire of theory-building itself. Every grand explanation, including Fort's own, is presented as provisional, playful and vulnerable to future contradiction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

For this reason, many historians describe Fort less as a prophet of the paranormal than as a literary sceptic. His invented cosmologies often read like parodies of the sweeping systems fashionable in his own era.

## The enduring argument

The lasting influence of *The Book of the Damned* lies less in any individual mystery than in its challenge to intellectual habits. Fort asked readers to distinguish between rejecting a claim because it has been thoroughly investigated and rejecting it because it falls outside familiar categories. That distinction has remained relevant across debates about scientific anomalies, historical evidence and institutional authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — ” In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, For...</span></span></span>

At the same time, the book demonstrates the limits of Fort's own approach. Collecting anomalies cannot by itself establish that extraordinary events occurred, and critics have long argued that his standards for evaluating evidence were inconsistent. Yet that weakness also reinforces the central irony of the work. Fort was less interested in replacing certainty with another certainty than in keeping certainty permanently unsettled. His greatest literary achievement was to transform a catalogue of odd reports into a sustained satire on humanity's confidence that it already understands the world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_Damned" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Book of the Damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Book of the Damned</span></span></span>
