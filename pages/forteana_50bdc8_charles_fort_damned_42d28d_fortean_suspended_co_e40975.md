## Introduction

A common misunderstanding is that being a Fortean means believing every strange story. [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})'s own legacy points in a more useful direction. His enduring contribution is not a catalogue of mysteries to accept, but a habit of resisting premature certainty. A Fortean reader neither dismisses unusual reports because they sound impossible nor accepts them simply because they are unexplained. Instead, they keep judgement open while demanding better evidence.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_charles_fort_damned_42d28d_fortean_suspended_co_e40975-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Suspended Belief illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_charles_fort_damned_42d28d_fortean_suspended_co_e40975-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_charles_fort_damned_42d28d_fortean_suspended_co_e40975-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
This attitude can be described as **suspended commitment**: recognising that an unexplained report is not the same thing as a proven phenomenon, while also recognising that an official explanation is not automatically the last word. Within the tradition of [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), this balance between curiosity and criticism is one of Fort's most practical and intellectually durable ideas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, Fort...</span></span></span>

## Can you be skeptical and Fortean?

The answer is yes—provided scepticism is understood as careful inquiry rather than automatic disbelief.

Fort repeatedly criticised what he saw as the tendency of institutions to discard awkward observations because they disrupted accepted theories. His complaint was directed less at science as a method than at the human habit of protecting established systems from inconvenient evidence. He argued that exclusion itself deserved examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Book of the Damned by Charles FortFort catalogs anomalous phenomena that mainstream science has excluded or &quot;damned&quot;...</span></span></span>

Modern Forteana has largely inherited this approach rather than a blanket belief in paranormal claims. Many writers describe themselves as interested in anomalies while remaining agnostic about what caused them. The question becomes, "What happened?" rather than "Which extraordinary explanation must be true?"<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fort" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Charles Fort</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charles Fort</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because it separates Fortean thinking from both unquestioning belief and reflexive debunking. A sceptical Fortean accepts uncertainty as a temporary position instead of treating uncertainty as a problem that must immediately be resolved.

## Unexplained is not proven

One of the easiest [mistakes]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}) in discussing anomalies is to confuse the absence of an explanation with evidence for a favourite explanation.

A strange light in the sky may remain unidentified for decades. That does not establish that it was an extraterrestrial spacecraft. Likewise, an unusual coincidence does not automatically imply hidden forces, psychic abilities or conspiracy.

Fort himself often offered deliberately extravagant speculative ideas, but scholars generally regard these less as settled beliefs than as rhetorical devices intended to expose overconfidence in supposedly complete explanations. His books frequently provoke readers into questioning certainty rather than accepting a replacement doctrine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, Fort...</span></span></span>

A disciplined Fortean reader therefore distinguishes between several very different claims:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A report exists.**
* **The report appears sincere or well documented.**
* **No satisfactory explanation has yet been established.**
* **A particular extraordinary explanation is correct.**

</div>

Only the first three may be justified by the available evidence. The fourth usually requires much stronger support.

Keeping these levels separate prevents mystery from becoming mythology.

## Explained is not always closed

The opposite mistake is assuming that every official explanation permanently settles a case.

History contains examples where accepted scientific or historical views changed after overlooked evidence received renewed attention. Fort's broader point was that institutions, like individuals, can become attached to prevailing assumptions. Reports may be ignored because they appear trivial, embarrassing or incompatible with current models rather than because they have been carefully investigated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — In rescuing the excluded, damned facts, Fort...</span></span></span>

That does not mean every rejected claim will eventually be vindicated. Most will not.

Instead, the Fortean lesson is procedural:

* Ask whether the evidence was actually examined.
* Distinguish [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}) from investigation.
* Revisit old assumptions when genuinely new evidence appears.
* Accept correction in either direction.

Sometimes further research strengthens an unusual claim. More often it reveals mundane causes such as observational error, [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}), hoaxes or misunderstood natural phenomena. Either outcome represents progress because both depend upon investigation rather than assumption.

## How Forteana uses disciplined doubt

Suspended commitment is best understood as a practical method rather than a philosophical slogan.

A disciplined Fortean reader typically follows several habits.

**Separate the report from the interpretation.** Witnesses can accurately report what they experienced while being mistaken about what produced it.

**Judge evidence, not expectations.** A bizarre claim should neither receive automatic acceptance nor automatic rejection solely because it conflicts with familiar beliefs.

**Look for independent confirmation.** Multiple reliable sources, physical evidence and contemporaneous documentation deserve more weight than repeated [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}) of the same story.

**Expect ordinary explanations first—but not exclusively.** Common causes explain most anomalies. Remaining open to less familiar possibilities does not require treating them as equally probable.

**Remain willing to change your mind.** Suspended commitment is temporary. Better evidence should eventually move the conclusion towards acceptance, rejection or continued uncertainty.

## The value of uncertainty

Many readers find uncertainty uncomfortable because it feels incomplete. Forteana instead treats uncertainty as an honest description of the current state of knowledge.

This attitude has two important consequences.

First, it reduces the temptation to invent explanations merely to eliminate ambiguity. A mystery is allowed to remain a mystery when the evidence does not justify stronger conclusions.

Second, it encourages continued [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}). An unresolved report is not a failed investigation; it is a reminder that knowledge develops over time, and that some questions remain open because the available evidence is genuinely insufficient.

In this sense, suspended commitment is less about paranormal belief than intellectual discipline.

## A Fortean approach to reading strange claims

Applied to newspapers, witness testimony or online discussions, suspended commitment encourages a simple sequence of questions:


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_charles_fort_damned_42d28d_fortean_suspended_co_e40975-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Suspended Belief illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_charles_fort_damned_42d28d_fortean_suspended_co_e40975-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_charles_fort_damned_42d28d_fortean_suspended_co_e40975-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. What is actually being claimed?
2. What evidence supports the claim?
3. Which parts are observations and which are interpretations?
4. Have plausible conventional explanations been properly considered?
5. Does the remaining uncertainty justify belief, disbelief or simply withholding judgement?

</div>

For Fort, the greatest danger was not being wrong about a particular mystery. It was becoming so certain that inconvenient evidence ceased to be visible.
