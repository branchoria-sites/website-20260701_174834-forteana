## Introduction

Fortean journalism is the newsroom side of forteana: the process by which an odd local report becomes a public story, a clipping, a rumour, a magazine item, an archive entry and, sometimes, a lasting legend. Its importance is not that [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}) prove monsters, UFOs or impossible rains. It is that news framing decides which strange claims are preserved, how cautiously they are described, who gets treated as credible, and whether uncertainty is clarified or made more exciting. [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }}) built his work from reported anomalies, and later Fortean outlets such as *Fortean Times* turned that habit into a continuing record of “weird world news”, reporting and research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain Review...</span></span></span>


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_fortean_journalism_s_f57056-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Strange News" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
The best Fortean journalism therefore sits between credulity and [dismissal]({{ 'dismissal/' | relative_url }}). It asks: what exactly was reported, by whom, where did the wording change, what ordinary checks were attempted, and what did later [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}) add? The weakest version simply packages ambiguity as mystery. The strongest version preserves strangeness without pretending that confusion is evidence.

## From Local Report to Wider Retelling

Many Fortean stories begin modestly: a witness speaks to a local reporter, a police officer, a small-town editor or a specialist newsletter. The first version often matters more than later legend because it fixes the vocabulary. A “thing”, a “creature”, a “flying saucer” or a “mystery noise” gives later writers a ready-made frame.

Charles Fort’s own method depended on this public trail. He collected reports from newspapers, magazines and scientific journals, filing anomalies that seemed to contradict confident explanations. Historian Charlotte Sleigh notes that Fort gathered astronomical newspaper clippings with particular energy, especially material that appeared to undercut accepted predictions or expert confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kar.kent.ac.uk/50907/1/C.%20Sleigh%20-%20An%20outcry%20of%20silence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kar.kent.ac.uk">[kar.kent.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kar.kent.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Silences of Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Silences of Science</span></span></span> Public Domain Review’s account of Fort’s *Book of the Damned* similarly stresses that his archive grew from “raining frogs, mysterious [disappearances]({{ 'disappearances/' | relative_url }}), baffling objects in the sky” and other reports he found in print.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain Review...</span></span></span>

That origin matters because Fortean history is often less a record of events than a record of reports about events. A local article may capture a witness’s immediate uncertainty, while later retellings tidy the story into a cleaner myth. The Fortean reader has to compare layers: first report, follow-up, national pickup, specialist retelling, sceptical explanation, anniversary feature and tourist-board afterlife.

The Mothman story shows this process clearly. In November 1966, the Point Pleasant case entered print through strikingly hesitant language: the local headline described a “man-sized bird”, “creature” and “something”, preserving the witnesses’ inability to categorise what they believed they had seen. The Smithsonian Center for Folklife and Cultural Heritage traces how the account moved from local fear and newspaper coverage into John Keel’s later book, a Hollywood film, a museum, a statue and a regional identity marker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/mothman-point-pleasant-west-virginia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklife.si.edu">[folklife.si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklife.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span> The case became Fortean not simply because people reported a winged figure, but because journalism gave the uncertainty a durable public form.

Warminster offers a British example. In 1965, Arthur Shuttlewood of the *Warminster Journal* reported local accounts of vibrations, menacing sounds and unexplained night-time sights. A photograph passed from local hands through Shuttlewood to the *Daily Mirror* helped push the “Warminster Thing” from town rumour into national UFO culture. A 2025 retrospective in the *Warminster Journal* quotes former Ministry of Defence investigator Nick Pope describing how the story gained “critical mass”: reports accumulated, mainstream media arrived, public meetings were held, and the sightings became “something else”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warminsterjournal.co.uk/ufo-expert-gives-verdict-on-warminster-thing-60-years-on/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warminsterjournal.co.uk">[Warminster Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warminsterjournal.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on warminsterjournal.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The pattern is central to Fortean journalism. A strange event rarely travels unchanged. It is translated at each stage:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Witness phrase to headline:** uncertainty becomes a memorable label.
* **Local story to national item:** a community disturbance becomes entertainment, threat or mystery.
* **News item to Fortean archive:** scattered reports become a case file.
* **Archive to legend:** later writers select the most vivid details and often drop the qualifying language.

</div>

A responsible Fortean account keeps those transformations visible.

## Headlines Can Preserve Doubt or Manufacture Mystery

Strange news is especially vulnerable to headline pressure. Editors need a concise hook; Fortean material offers ready-made novelty. But the choice between “residents report unexplained lights” and “UFO terror over town” is not cosmetic. It changes the reader’s evidential starting point.

The Mothman headline is memorable because it does not fully settle the category: “bird”, “creature”, “something”. That uncertainty is good Fortean evidence because it tells us the original report was unstable. Later versions that call the figure simply “Mothman” make the case easier to remember but also more creature-like than the first wording required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/mothman-point-pleasant-west-virginia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklife.si.edu">[folklife.si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklife.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

Warminster shows the opposite risk: repeated reports, public meetings and national attention can turn a cluster of ambiguous observations into a branded mystery. Pope’s retrospective comments are useful precisely because they do not flatten the story into either proof or debunking. He points to possible explanations such as military exercises, flares and meteorological activity, while also noting that nobody gave local people a definitive explanation at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warminsterjournal.co.uk/ufo-expert-gives-verdict-on-warminster-thing-60-years-on/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warminsterjournal.co.uk">[Warminster Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warminsterjournal.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on warminsterjournal.co.uk.</span></span></span> That gap is where Fortean history grows: not from confirmed impossibility, but from a failure of timely, trusted explanation.

Modern misinformation research helps explain why strange news travels so well. MIT’s summary of a major *Science* study found that false news on Twitter spread farther, faster and more broadly than true news, with false stories 70 per cent more likely to be retweeted in the dataset examined. The researchers also found that people, not only bots, drove the faster spread.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.mit.edu/2018/study-twitter-false-news-travels-faster-true-stories-0308" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.mit.edu">[MIT News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.mit.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mit.edu.</span></span></span> Fortean stories are not automatically false, but they often contain the ingredients that make stories shareable: novelty, surprise, emotional charge and unresolved interpretation.

This creates a special duty for editors. Strange-news [headlines]({{ 'headlines/' | relative_url }}) should avoid pretending that a witness report is a verified phenomenon. A headline can be vivid without overclaiming: “Residents report booming sky noises” is more honest than “mystery weapon shakes town” unless there is evidence for a weapon. The Fortean value is not weakened by caution. It is strengthened, because later readers can see what was actually known.

## Specialist Outlets Turn Clippings into a Continuing Record

Fortean journalism is not only mainstream news accidentally producing oddities. It also includes specialist publications that deliberately collect, compare and revisit strange reports. *Fortean Times* is the most visible example in Britain. The *Guardian* described it in 2002 as the “journal of strange phenomena”, filled with UFOs, crop circles and apparitions, and noted that it was founded in 1973 to continue Fort’s work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/media/2002/nov/04/pressandpublishing3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The magazine’s role is important because a clipping by itself is fragile. Specialist Fortean outlets preserve small items that would otherwise vanish: odd animal falls, unusual coincidences, local apparitions, anomalous lights, strange archaeological claims and corrections to earlier reports. They also create a community of readers who send in clippings, challenge explanations and notice repeated motifs.

That archival function can clarify confusion. A specialist editor may recognise that a “mystery light” resembles Chinese lantern reports from a particular period, that a “monster” description echoes known folklore, or that a dramatic claim has been exaggerated in syndication. The same function can also amplify confusion if an item is repeated for entertainment without source checking, date control or clear distinction between allegation, witness memory and documentation.

The best specialist Fortean journalism therefore has two jobs at once. It keeps the odd item alive long enough to be examined, and it prevents the item from hardening too quickly into a belief. That is a difficult balance. A publication devoted to strange phenomena must remain interesting, but it loses value if every ambiguity is treated as confirmation.

The *Fortean Times* editorial tradition has often been described as a mixture of weird news, research and sceptical humour rather than a single doctrine. The *Guardian* quoted incoming editor David Sutton promising to bring more readers to the magazine’s mix of “weird world news, in-depth reporting and research into the more outlandish corners of human experience”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/media/2002/nov/04/pressandpublishing3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span> That phrase captures the useful niche: not just paranormal belief, and not just debunking, but a record of how strange claims appear in public.

## Official Files Show Why Reporting Systems Matter

Fortean journalism often overlaps with official reporting systems. UFO files are the clearest example, because governments, militaries and archives sometimes collect public reports while making no paranormal claim about them.

The United States Air Force’s Project Blue Book investigated UFO reports from 1947 to 1969. Its fact sheet says 12,618 sightings were reported, 701 remained “unidentified”, and the Air Force concluded that no investigated UFO was shown to be a national-security threat, an advanced technology beyond scientific knowledge, or an extraterrestrial vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/ufo/usaf_fact_sheet_95_03.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">usaf fact sheet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">usaf fact sheet</span></span></span> That distinction is vital for responsible reporting: “unidentified” means not resolved within the system, not automatically alien, paranormal or impossible.

The United Kingdom’s records show a similar reporting problem. GOV.UK lists Ministry of Defence UFO reports from 1997 to 2009, with dates, times, locations and brief descriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ufo-reports-in-the-uk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O reports in the UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UF O reports in the UK</span></span></span> The National Archives’ 2013 release transcript says the MoD closed its UFO desk and hotline in November 2009, ending almost 60 years of collecting, analysing and sometimes investigating sky-sighting reports. It also notes that 2009 produced a record 643 sightings up to the closure, with many recent reports apparently involving ordinary objects such as Chinese lanterns photographed as blobs of light against dark skies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ufo-video-transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UF O file release video transcript</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UF O file release video transcript</span></span></span>

For Fortean journalism, these files are not boring bureaucracy. They show what happens when a society creates an intake channel for anomalies. People report more; reports cluster around media attention; weak images accumulate; officials must decide whether to investigate, file, redirect or stop collecting. The archive then becomes part of the mystery culture it was meant to manage.

This is why implementation matters. A reporting system that records time, location, direction, duration, weather, witness position, images, prior media exposure and possible conventional explanations is far more useful than one that merely logs “UFO seen”. Likewise, a newspaper that tells readers what checks were made creates a better Fortean record than one that simply repeats a dramatic claim.

## Responsible Anomaly Reporting

Responsible Fortean journalism does not require hostility to witnesses. Many people sincerely report things they cannot explain. The ethical task is to protect both the witness and the reader: neither ridicule nor unearned validation is good reporting.

Mainstream verification standards apply directly to strange news. Reuters’ standards say online reporting requires the same sourcing, identification and verification as other newsgathering, and warn against using material that cannot be verified as to origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reutersagency.com">[reutersagency.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reutersagency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reuters Journalistic Standards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reuters Journalistic Standards</span></span></span> The *Verification Handbook*, written for journalists and aid responders dealing with user-generated content, stresses the need to sift, verify and report accurate information when many reports are circulating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://verificationhandbook.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: verificationhandbook.com">[verificationhandbook.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">verificationhandbook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Verification Handbook: homepage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Verification Handbook: homepage</span></span></span> These principles are especially useful for Fortean stories because many begin as witness testimony, mobile-phone images, local rumour or recycled archive material.

A practical policy for anomaly reporting should include these habits:

1. **State the claim at the right level.** Say “a witness reported a light” rather than “a UFO appeared” unless the only claim is that the object was unidentified.
2. **Separate [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) from interpretation.** “Three orange lights moved silently” is an observation claim; “alien craft” is an interpretation.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

3. **Preserve uncertainty in the wording.** Doubt is not a flaw in a Fortean report. It is part of the evidence.
4. **Record conditions.** Time, weather, location, direction, distance, duration, sound, other witnesses and camera settings often matter more than the dramatic description.
5. **Check mundane explanations early.** Aircraft, drones, lanterns, astronomical objects, animals, reflections, military exercises, weather, hoaxes and misdated images should be considered before mystery framing.
6. **Avoid ridicule.** Mockery drives witnesses away and damages the record. Careful scepticism works better than contempt.
7. **Update visibly.** If an explanation emerges, the correction should be attached to the original story, not buried elsewhere.
8. **Track retellings.** A Fortean article should say whether a detail comes from the first report, a later interview, a book, a film, a museum display or internet folklore.

</div>

This approach does not drain the subject of wonder. It makes wonder more durable. A story that survives careful description is more valuable than one that depends on ambiguity being inflated.

## When Strange News Becomes Fortean History

Strange news becomes Fortean history when it outlives its first news cycle. That afterlife may take the form of an archive, a museum, a festival, a specialist magazine file, a sceptical reassessment, a government release or a community legend. Point Pleasant’s Mothman has become a case study in local identity as much as alleged creature sighting; the Smithsonian notes that cryptid stories can promote pride of place and that Point Pleasant now has a museum, statue and tourism economy around the legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/mothman-point-pleasant-west-virginia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklife.si.edu">[folklife.si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklife.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on si.edu.</span></span></span>

The same transformation can happen without a creature at its centre. Warminster’s “Thing” remains part of the town’s memory because the story involved local witnesses, a local journalist, national media, public gatherings and decades of retrospective debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://warminsterjournal.co.uk/ufo-expert-gives-verdict-on-warminster-thing-60-years-on/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: warminsterjournal.co.uk">[Warminster Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">warminsterjournal.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on warminsterjournal.co.uk.</span></span></span> The Fortean record is made not only by what people saw, but by how institutions, newspapers and communities reacted to not knowing.

This is the heart of Fortean journalism. It does not merely report the strange; it helps decide what kind of strangeness the public inherits. A careful report leaves later readers with a usable trail: original language, source quality, competing explanations and cultural context. A careless report leaves only a slogan.

For forteana, the difference is decisive. The responsible strange-news writer does not have to solve every mystery. But they do have to keep the mystery honest.
