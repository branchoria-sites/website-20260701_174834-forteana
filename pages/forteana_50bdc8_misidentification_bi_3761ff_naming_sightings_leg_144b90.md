## Introduction

Many Fortean mysteries do not become enduring legends at the moment of the sighting. They become legends when someone gives the [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}) a recognisable name. An uncertain glimpse of a large animal becomes a "Big Cat". An odd light becomes a "UFO". A shadowy figure becomes a "ghost". Once a label exists, later witnesses have a ready-made category into which they can place their own ambiguous experiences. The result is that isolated reports begin to look like connected evidence, even when the original observations were unrelated or poorly understood. This process does not require fraud or mass delusion. It emerges from ordinary human habits of categorisation, memory and storytelling, all of which shape how unusual events are recalled and shared. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8076587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These failures and distortions are influenced by several...</span></span></span>

## From encounter to first telling

A strange experience usually begins as something difficult to classify rather than something obviously paranormal. A witness may notice movement in poor light, hear an unfamiliar sound or glimpse an unusual silhouette. At that stage, the experience often contains uncertainty rather than a complete narrative.

The first retelling is therefore crucial. As the witness searches for words, they naturally compare the event with familiar ideas. Instead of saying "I saw something I could not identify," they may say, "It looked like a ghost," or "It reminded me of a flying saucer." These comparisons are not necessarily claims of certainty. They are attempts to communicate an unfamiliar experience using shared language.

Folklorists distinguish between a personal experience narrative and a fully developed legend. A first-person account, often called a memorate, may begin as an individual's sincere description of an unusual event. As that account is repeated, simplified and linked to other stories, it can gradually lose its individuality and become part of a wider legendary tradition. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorate" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## How labels sharpen ambiguous details

Naming does more than organise stories. It changes how people remember them.

Research on eyewitness memory consistently shows that recollection is reconstructive rather than photographic. Memories are influenced by later information, discussions with other people and the concepts available to describe an event. Once a witness adopts a label, later recall often becomes organised around that category instead of around the original sensory experience. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8076587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2ojs.pensamultimedia.it]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These failures and distortions are influenced by several...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why later [retellings]({{ 'retellings/' | relative_url }}) often appear more detailed than the original account. A witness who initially recalled only "a large dark shape" may later remember wings, glowing eyes or impossible movement because those features fit the accepted image associated with the chosen label. The process is usually unconscious rather than deceptive.

Recent cognitive research also suggests that verbal labels become integrated with memory during learning and consolidation. Giving an event a meaningful title or category can influence how it is later reconstructed, making the remembered event more consistent with the concept than with the original perception. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/influences-of-learned-verbal-labels-and-sleep-on-temporal-event-m/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.york.ac.uk">[Pure York]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.york.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">influences of learned verbal labels and sleep on temporal event m</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pure YorkInfluences of learned verbal labels and sleep on temporal...by Y Wang · 2024 · Cited by 3 — Conceptual knowledge is known to mo...</span></span></span>

## Why named categories attract later reports

Once a community recognises a category, later ambiguous sightings become easier to classify in the same way.

This creates a feedback loop:

* A report receives a memorable label.
* The label circulates through conversation, [newspapers]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}) or online communities.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* People encountering similarly ambiguous events recognise the label.
* New reports reinforce the apparent existence of the category.
* The growing collection of reports makes the category appear increasingly established.

</div>

The important point is that the label itself becomes part of the environment through which later witnesses interpret their experiences. Instead of confronting an entirely unknown event, they confront something that already has a cultural identity.

This mechanism helps explain why reports often cluster around well-known Fortean categories rather than producing an unlimited variety of unique mysteries. People are more likely to describe a mysterious light as a UFO than invent an entirely new class of aerial phenomenon, because "UFO" already provides a familiar framework for interpretation. ## When official language changes the legend

Names introduced by investigators can also reshape public perception.

One notable example is the replacement of the popular phrase "flying saucer" with the more neutral term "unidentified flying object". During investigations such as [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book-75a50c/' | relative_url }}), officials preferred terminology that described an object as unidentified rather than assuming its shape or origin. The new label broadened the category dramatically, allowing reports of lights, discs, triangles and many other aerial phenomena to be grouped together under a single heading. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Blue_Book" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book</span></span></span>

The choice of words mattered. "Flying saucer" encouraged witnesses to imagine a particular form, whereas "UFO" invited almost any unexplained aerial [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) into the same reporting tradition. More recently, the term "unidentified anomalous phenomena" (UAP) has similarly expanded the language used in official investigations, showing how changing terminology can alter what people consider relevant to report. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Blue_Book" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book</span></span></span>

## Why repeated names create the illusion of independent evidence

A named category can make separate reports appear more closely related than they really are.

If ten witnesses independently report "a strange animal", investigators may initially treat the cases cautiously because the descriptions differ. If the same ten reports are all described publicly as sightings of a specific cryptid, readers may perceive them as mutually supporting evidence, even when the observations vary greatly in appearance, behaviour and circumstances.

This is not simply a matter of media exaggeration. Human beings naturally group similar experiences into categories. Once enough reports share the same label, the category itself becomes a lens through which future evidence is interpreted.

Folklore researchers have long observed similar processes in the development of legends. Personal accounts become linked to established narratives, while familiar narrative patterns encourage people to interpret new experiences in comparable ways. Over time, the legend becomes more stable than any individual sighting that originally contributed to it. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/12856472/Memorates_and_memory_A_re_evaluation_of_Lauri_Honko_s_theory_2015_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia+2Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">It confronts psychological knowledge about</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Memorates and memory: A re-evaluation of Lauri Honko&#x27;s theory...This paper deals with the phenomena where culture and society in...</span></span></span>

## Naming as the bridge between perception and legend

Within [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), naming is one of the most powerful mechanisms connecting ordinary perception to extraordinary belief. The original sighting may last only seconds and remain deeply uncertain. The name, however, can persist for decades.

By giving an ambiguous encounter a recognised identity, communities make it easier to remember, compare and repeat. Each new report is then interpreted against earlier named examples, creating an expanding tradition that often feels more coherent than the underlying evidence alone.
