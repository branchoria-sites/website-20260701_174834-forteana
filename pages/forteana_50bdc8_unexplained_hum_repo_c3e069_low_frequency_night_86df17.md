## Introduction

Many reports of unexplained hums share one striking feature: the sound seems most intrusive inside the home after dark. This pattern does not necessarily point to a mysterious source. Low-frequency sound behaves differently from higher-pitched noise, and ordinary buildings can change how it is experienced. At the same time, the quieter acoustic environment of night makes faint sounds easier to notice, while sleep disruption can increase awareness and distress. These interacting mechanisms help explain why a hum that is barely noticeable outdoors can seem overwhelming in a bedroom, even when investigators struggle to identify a single obvious source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ioa.org.uk/dam/jcr%3Aad9cf740-e84b-4cd4-908a-eb8b484dd5b2/briefing_note_low_frequency_noise_annoyance_0.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ioa.org.uk">[Institute of Acoustics+2LWW Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ioa.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of AcousticsLow Frequency Noise (LFN) AnnoyanceLFN can travel long distances, so sources of low frequency noise are often chall...</span></span></span>

Within the broader history of unexplained hum reports, this indoor night-time pattern is one of the most consistent observations. Understanding how buildings, room acoustics and human perception interact provides a more useful explanation than assuming that every case reflects either a hidden industrial source or an entirely psychological [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}).

## How low frequencies move through buildings

Low-frequency sound, generally below about 200 Hz, has wavelengths measured in metres rather than centimetres. Because of these long wavelengths, it behaves differently from speech or birdsong. Instead of being easily blocked by walls, it can pass through building structures, travel around obstacles and excite vibrations in floors, ceilings and window frames.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.lww.com/nohe/fulltext/2004/06230/low_frequency_noise_and_annoyance.6.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.lww.com">[LWW Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.lww.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">low frequency noise and annoyance.6.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LWW JournalsLow Frequency Noise and Annoyanceby HG Leventhall · 2004 · Cited by 472 — Low frequency noise, considered as the frequency ra...</span></span></span>

This means that a distant source such as large ventilation equipment, pumps, compressors, industrial machinery or heavy transport may be more noticeable indoors than expected. Buildings do not simply keep sound out; under some circumstances they can transmit or redistribute low-frequency energy in ways that make it appear stronger in particular rooms. The Institute of Acoustics notes that these characteristics are one reason low-frequency noise complaints are often difficult to investigate and trace to a single source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ioa.org.uk/dam/jcr%3Aad9cf740-e84b-4cd4-908a-eb8b484dd5b2/briefing_note_low_frequency_noise_annoyance_0.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ioa.org.uk">[Institute of Acoustics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ioa.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute of AcousticsLow Frequency Noise (LFN) AnnoyanceLFN can travel long distances, so sources of low frequency noise are often chall...</span></span></span>

Another complication is that low-frequency sound is difficult for people to localise. Human hearing relies partly on differences between the ears to determine where sound originates, but those cues become much weaker at very low frequencies. As a result, listeners often describe the hum as coming from "everywhere", from inside the walls, or from no obvious direction at all. Recent research into persistent hum perception highlights this difficulty in localisation as one of the defining characteristics of reported cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.popularmechanics.com/science/health/a71629139/the-hum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: popularmechanics.com">[Popular Mechanics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">popularmechanics.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Popular Mechanics A Mysterious Hum Has Plagued the Earth for 50 Years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scientists May Have Finally Found Its Source.June 25, 2026 — For over 50 years, a mysterious low-frequency noise known as &quot;the Hum&quot; has b...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why rooms can create misleading hotspots

A room is not an acoustically neutral space. Reflections from walls, ceilings and floors can reinforce particular low frequencies while reducing them elsewhere. ## Why night-time quiet changes perception

The physical environment changes significantly after dark. Traffic falls, neighbourhood activity decreases and household appliances are switched off. As the general background noise level drops, faint low-frequency sounds that were previously masked become easier to hear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.acoustics.asn.au/conference_proceedings/AAS2017/papers/p14.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: acoustics.asn.au">[acoustics.asn.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">acoustics.asn.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Determinants of annoyance from humming sound as...by I van Kamp · 2017 · Cited by 10 — This is in line with the notion that low frequenc...</span></span></span>

This does not necessarily mean the hum itself has become louder. Instead, the contrast between the hum and its surroundings has increased. A steady drone that is effectively hidden during a busy afternoon may become the dominant sound in an otherwise quiet bedroom.

Night also changes the listener. In quiet surroundings people naturally become more aware of subtle sensory input, particularly when trying to fall asleep. Once attention focuses on a repetitive sound, it becomes difficult to ignore. Repeated nights of disturbed sleep can further increase sensitivity and frustration, creating a cycle in which anticipation of the hum makes it easier to notice. Research on low-frequency noise consistently identifies sleep disturbance and annoyance as among its most important consequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15273026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of low frequency noise on sleepby KP Waye · 2004 · Cited by 94 — Sleep disturbance, especially with regard to time to fall...</span></span></span>

## Why not everyone in the same house hears it

One of the most puzzling aspects of unexplained hum reports is that different members of the same household often disagree about whether any sound exists.

Several mechanisms may contribute:

* **Different hearing sensitivity.** People's ability to detect very low frequencies varies considerably, especially at frequencies near the limits of hearing. Some individuals are substantially more sensitive than average.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.lww.com/nohe/fulltext/2004/06230/low_frequency_noise_and_annoyance.6.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.lww.com">[LWW Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.lww.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">low frequency noise and annoyance.6.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LWW JournalsLow Frequency Noise and Annoyanceby HG Leventhall · 2004 · Cited by 472 — Low frequency noise, considered as the frequency ra...</span></span></span>
* **Different listening positions.** Because room modes create acoustic hotspots, two people sleeping in different rooms—or even different parts of the same room—may experience different sound levels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.soton.ac.uk/soundwaves/standing-waves/5-room-modes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.soton.ac.uk">[blog.soton.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.soton.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Room modes &#124; Sound WavesRoom modes. ## Why indoor hum reports remain difficult to resolve

The indoor, night-time character of many hum reports creates practical challenges for investigators.

Standard environmental noise assessments often rely on overall sound levels weighted to match average human hearing. However, these methods can underestimate the importance of very low frequencies, especially when the sound is tonal, intermittent or confined to particular indoor locations. Researchers and professional acousticians have long argued that low-frequency complaints require specialised measurement techniques and careful interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.lww.com/nohe/fulltext/2004/06230/low_frequency_noise_and_annoyance.6.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.lww.com">[LWW Journals+2Institute of Acoustics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.lww.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">low frequency noise and annoyance.6.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LWW JournalsLow Frequency Noise and Annoyanceby HG Leventhall · 2004 · Cited by 472 — Low frequency noise, considered as the frequency ra...</span></span></span>

Even when measurable low-frequency sound is present, linking it conclusively to a single source may be difficult because several weak sources can combine, atmospheric conditions can alter sound propagation, and buildings themselves modify the sound field. In other cases, no corresponding external source can be identified, leaving open the possibility that internal auditory mechanisms contribute to the experience.

For Fortean discussions, this is precisely why the indoor night-time hum remains compelling. It occupies an uncertain middle ground where real acoustic behaviour, individual perception and incomplete evidence intersect. The experience reported by witnesses is often genuine and distressing, yet the mechanisms producing it may differ from one household to another, making the phenomenon resistant to simple explanations.
