## Introduction

*Fortean Times* occupies a distinctive place in [Fortean journalism]({{ 'strange-news/' | relative_url }}) because it treats odd [newspaper clippings]({{ 'newspapers/' | relative_url }}) as the beginning of an investigation rather than the end of one. Following the example of Charles Fort, who built his books from thousands of reports gathered from newspapers, journals and scientific publications, the magazine preserves unusual stories that might otherwise disappear while encouraging readers to ask where they came from, how they changed over time and whether later evidence supports or weakens them. This approach gives seemingly trivial local reports lasting historical value without assuming that every mystery is genuine. At its best, a clipped oddity becomes a traceable document in the history of an unexplained claim rather than a piece of folklore detached from its sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — Rains of blood and frogs, mysterious disappea...</span></span></span>

## How specialist outlets preserve fragile reports

Before searchable online archives, many unusual local stories had remarkably short lives. A report about a strange animal, unexplained lights or an odd [weather]({{ 'weather/' | relative_url }}) event might appear in a provincial newspaper and then vanish from public view within days. Enthusiasts clipped these articles, exchanged them by post and gradually assembled collections that preserved material larger newspapers and libraries often ignored.

*Fortean Times*, founded in 1973 from Bob Rickard's newsletter *The News*, inherited this tradition while giving it a more systematic form. Rather than relying solely on dramatic feature articles, it created regular sections that gathered short reports from newspapers around the world. Many of these items were supplied by readers, who sent original clippings or references to local publications. This crowdsourced archive helped preserve stories that would otherwise have been difficult to rediscover years later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortean_Times" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fortean Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fortean Times</span></span></span>

The value of this practice extends beyond paranormal interests. Local newspapers frequently record eyewitness descriptions before later retellings simplify, exaggerate or fictionalise events. Even when an unusual report ultimately receives an [ordinary explanation]({{ 'ordinary-causes/' | relative_url }}), the original clipping remains useful evidence of how the event was first understood and presented.

## Why a clipping is evidence of reporting, not proof of the event

One of the most important Fortean habits is separating the existence of a report from the truth of the reported event.

A newspaper clipping demonstrates that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* someone made a claim;
* an editor considered it newsworthy;
* specific wording entered the public record;
* later writers can compare their versions against the earliest published account.

</div>

It does **not** automatically demonstrate that the underlying event occurred exactly as described.

This distinction mirrors [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})'s own method. He collected anomalies because they challenged assumptions about certainty, not because every report deserved unquestioning belief. Modern *Fortean Times* has repeatedly described its editorial stance as one of "benevolent scepticism", aiming neither to dismiss unusual claims automatically nor to promote them without examination. Original sourcing therefore matters as much as the strange claim itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/charles-fort-and-the-book-of-the-damned/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charles fort and the book of the damned</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Public Domain ReviewStrange Gods: Charles Fort&#x27;s Book of the Damned (1919)26 Nov 2024 — Rains of blood and frogs, mysterious disappea...</span></span></span>

## Reader tips, corrections and recurring motifs

The magazine's long-running interaction with readers is one reason clipped oddities retain research value instead of becoming a simple catalogue of curiosities.

Readers contribute by:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* locating forgotten newspaper reports;
* identifying the original publication behind widely repeated stories;
* correcting dates, locations and quotations;
* supplying follow-up coverage that may explain or complicate an earlier mystery;
* recognising recurring legends that have appeared repeatedly under different names.

</div>

This collaborative approach has practical consequences. A mysterious story that appears unique may later prove to have circulated decades earlier with only minor changes. Conversely, several apparently unrelated reports may reveal a consistent historical pattern once their original sources are assembled.

An early feature, **[Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}) Corrigenda**, was specifically intended to identify errors that had accumulated in Fortean literature through repeated quotation and misattribution. That emphasis on correction reflects an understanding that copying without verification gradually transforms uncertain reports into apparently established facts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortean_Times" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fortean Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fortean Times</span></span></span>

## The risk of repeating mystery without checks

The same clipping that preserves valuable evidence can also preserve mistakes.

Several problems commonly emerge when unusual reports are copied repeatedly:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* headlines become more dramatic than the underlying article;
* witness uncertainty disappears in later summaries;
* explanatory follow-up stories are omitted;
* separate incidents become merged into a single legend;
* secondary books quote one another instead of the original newspaper.

</div>

These changes can make a routine local curiosity appear stronger than the historical record supports. The problem is not unique to Fortean subjects, but it is especially important where dramatic narratives attract repeated retelling.

Specialist publications reduce this risk when they cite original newspapers, distinguish confirmed facts from reported claims and publish corrections when new information appears. They increase it when unattributed anecdotes are reproduced simply because they fit an appealing mystery.

## Why clipped oddities still matter

A single strange clipping rarely settles an unexplained event, but large collections reveal something more valuable: patterns in how societies report anomalies.

Across decades of *Fortean Times*, readers can trace recurring themes including mysterious animals, unusual weather, folklore, UFO reports, bizarre accidents, mistaken identities, [urban legends]({{ 'urban-legends/' | relative_url }}) and stories later exposed as hoaxes. Looking across hundreds of reports allows researchers to compare language, media framing and cultural assumptions rather than treating every case in isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortean_Times" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fortean Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fortean Times</span></span></span>

The accumulated archive also helps distinguish genuinely unusual incidents from recurring storytelling traditions. When a modern report resembles dozens of earlier newspaper accounts, researchers gain context that would be impossible from a single clipping alone.

## Reading Fortean clippings critically

The most useful way to approach a clipped oddity is neither to accept nor reject it immediately, but to ask practical questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Can the original newspaper or publication be identified?
* Is the clipping complete, or only a headline or extract?
* Were follow-up reports published?
* Do independent contemporary sources describe the same event?
* Has the story changed in later books, documentaries or online retellings?
* Were corrections or alternative explanations published afterwards?

</div>

Approached in this way, clipped oddities become historical documents rather than isolated curiosities. Their lasting value lies less in proving extraordinary events than in preserving fragile records of how extraordinary claims entered—and sometimes evolved within—the public record.

      if (navigator.languages && navigator.languages.length) languages = Array.prototype.slice.call(navigator.languages);
      else if (navigator.language) languages = [navigator.language];
    } catch (err) {}

      if (!normalized) continue;
      if (localeMarketMap[normalized]) {

        break;
      }

      if (!languageMarket && localeMarketMap[languageOnly]) languageMarket = localeMarketMap[languageOnly];
    }

  }

    var matches = localeMatches();

  }
  function inferFromTimezone() {
    var tz = '';

    if (!tz) return '';
    for (var i = 0; i< timezoneRules.length; i += 1) {
      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {
        if (new RegExp(rule.pattern).test(tz)) return rule.market;
      } catch (err) {}
    }
    return '';
  }

    available = Array.isArray(available) ? available : [];
    options = options || {};

    if (localeMarkets.language && available.indexOf(localeMarkets.language) >= 0) return localeMarkets.language;

    return available[0] || '';
  }

  };
