## Introduction

Fortean taxonomies are useful because the field is too wide to navigate as a single heap of oddities. A reader needs some way to distinguish strange falls, anomalous lights, cryptid reports, hauntings, miracle claims, psychical experiences, unusual deaths and modern unidentified anomalous phenomena. Yet the moment those categories become too tidy, they can erase the very thing that makes a Fortean case worth noticing: its refusal to behave like a normal specimen in a normal drawer.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_fortean_taxonomies_w_7e6998-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Taxonomies" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The point of a good Fortean taxonomy is therefore not to “solve” every case by naming its shelf. It is to govern uncertainty without killing it. [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})’s own starting point was not a neat paranormal map but a protest against excluded data: in *The Book of the Damned*, he described his “damned” facts as data that science had excluded, and his examples crossed boundaries between odd weather, strange falls, objects in the sky, apparitions, [disappearances]({{ 'disappearances/' | relative_url }}) and physical curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/22472/22472-h/22472-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> A Fortean taxonomy works best when it helps readers compare cases, track patterns and test explanations, while still preserving ambiguity, overlap and the possibility that a case matters precisely because it belongs in more than one box.

## Why Categories Help

Classification is not the enemy of [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}). Without categories, the field becomes a blur: a raining-fish report, a phantom big cat sighting and a radar-visual UAP report all become merely “weird”. That may be entertaining, but it is poor investigation. Categories let readers ask sharper questions: Was there a physical trace? Was the report first-hand or retold? Was the phenomenon seen by multiple witnesses? Did it resemble a known folklore pattern, a misidentified natural event, an instrument error, a hoax, or a genuinely unresolved [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }})?

This is why later Fortean and anomalistics traditions keep producing organising schemes. The Society for Psychical Research’s Psi Encyclopedia describes Forteana as ranging across strange falls, [cryptids]({{ 'cryptids/' | relative_url }}), UFOs, [miracles]({{ 'miracles/' | relative_url }}) and balls of light, while anomalistics is often framed as the systematic study of anomaly claims that do not fit accepted scientific pictures of reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/forteana/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Forteana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Forteana</span></span></span> The point is not that all those things are equally credible. It is that a reader or researcher needs a way to separate types of claim before judging them.

A useful taxonomy also protects weak cases from being over-promoted. If a reported “monster” is really an out-of-place animal panic, that belongs in a different analytical stream from a zoological claim about an undiscovered breeding population. Folklore scholarship on mystery animal appearances has shown how reports of large cats or “beasts” can become regional legends when they violate familiar boundaries between wild and domestic, local and exotic, ordinary and threatening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241722290_Appearances_of_Beasts_and_Mystery-Cats_in_France" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Appearances of Beasts and Mystery-Cats in France</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate Appearances of Beasts and Mystery-Cats in France</span></span></span> A label such as “phantom big cat” does not prove the animal exists, but it does gather comparable reports and invite better questions about ecology, media spread, witness description and local fear.

In UFO research, J. Allen Hynek’s close-encounter scale shows the practical benefit of classification. Hynek separated distant sightings into forms such as nocturnal lights, daylight discs and radar-visual reports, then distinguished closer cases according to whether they involved simple observation, physical effects, or reported entities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Close encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Close encounter</span></span></span> The scheme became famous partly through popular culture, but its investigative value lies in something quieter: it tells researchers which claims require which kinds of evidence. A light in the sky, a landing trace and an entity encounter should not be evaluated as though they were the same evidential problem.

Categories also make archives usable. *Fortean Times*, founded as *The News* in 1973 and renamed in 1976, developed regular streams for ghosts, cryptozoology, UFOs, odd deaths, archaeological curiosities, reader experiences and sceptical myth-checking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortean_Times" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fortean Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fortean Times</span></span></span> That editorial architecture matters because Forteana is cumulative. Old clippings, local reports and marginal observations become more useful when they can be compared across time rather than rediscovered as isolated curiosities.

## Where Categories Break Down

The trouble begins when a category is treated as an explanation. “Ghost”, “UFO”, “cryptid” or “miracle” often names a puzzle, not a cause. A ghost report may be a bereavement experience, a local legend, a hoax, a misperception, an architectural effect, a sleep-related event, a religious interpretation, or a story that has changed in retelling. Academic discussion often prefers narrower terms such as “apparitional experience” because “ghost” already implies a theory about survival after death, while the experience itself may admit several competing explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparitional_experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Apparitional experience</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Apparitional experience</span></span></span>

The same problem appears in aerial cases. “UFO” once sounded like a descriptive label, but it acquired such strong extraterrestrial associations that official and scientific discussions increasingly use “UAP” to avoid importing popular assumptions. NASA’s 2023 UAP independent study stressed that many cases are difficult to analyse because of poor sensor calibration, missing metadata, lack of multiple measurements and inadequate baseline data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Independent Study Team Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Independent Study Team Report</span></span></span> In other words, the label “unidentified” is often a statement about the available information, not about the nature of the object.

Rigid categories also flatten mixed cases. Fort’s own catalogues regularly moved between physical oddities, sky phenomena, animal falls, psychic-seeming events and absurd coincidences. Project Gutenberg’s summary of *The Book of the Damned* lists UFO-like sightings, fish and frog falls, poltergeist activity, unexplained disappearances and evidence for giants and fairies under the same broad project of excluded anomalies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> That unruliness is not a defect to be edited away. It is part of Fort’s method: he wanted to show that official knowledge often sorted inconvenient reports out of sight before they could trouble the system.

The category can even become a social identity. Cryptozoology, for example, can be approached as a zoological claim, a folklore pattern, a media phenomenon, a local identity marker, a tourist economy, or a pseudoscientific subculture. Mainstream reference and scholarly sources distinguish cryptozoology from academic zoology, while still noting that the term “cryptid” has become central to the subculture’s way of rebranding older monster traditions in more scientific-sounding language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptozoology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span> A rigid “animal/not animal” sorting misses the cultural work these creatures do in communities.

A final failure mode is premature debunking by taxonomy. Once a case is filed as “folklore”, some readers assume it has no empirical content. Once filed as “UFO”, others assume extraterrestrial technology. Once filed as “mass hysteria”, witness experience may be dismissed too quickly. Fortean governance needs a category system that slows down these leaps rather than accelerates them.

## How Mixed Cases Should Be Handled

Mixed Fortean cases should be handled with layered classification. Instead of asking “What is it?” first, a better taxonomy asks several smaller questions: what was reported, what evidence exists, how the story travelled, what explanations have been proposed, and what remains unresolved. This keeps the case investigable without pretending its strangeness has vanished.

A practical Fortean record can classify a case along four axes:

* **Phenomenon type:** light, sound, apparition, animal, object, bodily effect, fall from the sky, coincidence, disappearance, trace, message, or environmental change.
* **Evidence type:** first-hand testimony, multiple witnesses, photograph, video, instrument data, physical trace, official document, newspaper clipping, folklore record, later retelling, or no recoverable source.
* **Interpretive frame:** natural anomaly, [misidentification]({{ 'mistakes/' | relative_url }}), hoax, legend, psychological experience, religious claim, parapsychological claim, cryptozoological claim, UAP claim, or unresolved mixed case.
* **Confidence status:** explained, probably explained, insufficient data, contested, unresolved, or category error.

This approach borrows the discipline of modern data governance without reducing Forteana to a sterile spreadsheet. Recent UAP work makes the governance lesson especially clear. NASA’s UAP report did not claim that better data would automatically remove every mystery; it argued that without calibrated sensors, metadata and repeated measurements, many reports cannot be assessed responsibly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Independent Study Team Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Independent Study Team Report</span></span></span> AARO, the US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, similarly presents UAP as a reporting and resolution problem across airborne, transmedium and other anomalous observations, using a data-driven framework rather than a single paranormal interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

The same layered method helps older Fortean material. A nineteenth-century newspaper story about frogs falling from the sky should not be filed only as “impossible weather” or “silly folklore”. It may need tags for meteorology, animal behaviour, newspaper culture, witness testimony, possible exaggeration and Fortean reception. A “black dog” sighting might need tags for animal report, apparition, road legend, local haunting tradition and possible misidentification. A “haunted house” with knocks, apparitions and moving objects may need separate entries for sound phenomena, witness context, physical claims, legend history and later embellishment.

Mixed classification also makes disagreement easier to see. One reader may treat a case as folklore; another may see a physical anomaly; a sceptic may see misperception; a believer may see paranormal agency. A good taxonomy records those interpretations as interpretations. It does not silently turn the most popular reading into the case itself.

## The Governance Problem Behind the Weirdness

The phrase “without losing the weirdness” is not just about style. It is a governance problem: who gets to decide the categories, what gets excluded, and what happens to cases that do not fit?

Fort’s “damned” facts were a direct attack on exclusion. He accused organised knowledge of rejecting awkward data because it threatened accepted systems, and opened *The Book of the Damned* with a procession of excluded observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/22472/22472-h/22472-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> Whether or not one accepts Fort’s claims, the governance issue remains real: archives, journals, newsrooms, agencies and fan communities all decide which reports are preserved, ridiculed, investigated, renamed or forgotten.

Modern anomalistics tries to make that boundary work more explicit. Marcello Truzzi’s account of anomalistics, as summarised in reference literature, framed the field as helping evaluate anomaly claims, improving scientific adjudication, building conceptual frameworks for categorising claims, and acting as a “friend of the court” in disputes over extraordinary evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalistics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span> That is a useful model for Fortean taxonomy: categories should not become a belief system. They should make adjudication more transparent.

This is especially important because Forteana lives between institutions and vernacular culture. Official bodies may need clean definitions for reporting and risk assessment. Folklorists may track story transmission. Journalists may need readable beats such as ghosts, monsters and UFOs. Enthusiasts may preserve clippings that no institution would bother to keep. Each system sees something and misses something. *Fortean Times* demonstrates the hybrid model: its regular departments create order, but its reader clippings, “it happened to me” accounts and case revisits preserve the messy, anecdotal texture that a purely official archive might discard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortean_Times" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fortean Times</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fortean Times</span></span></span>

A good governance rule is therefore simple: separate filing from verdict. Filing a report under “cryptid” should mean “this is an animal-like claim whose existence is disputed or unconfirmed”, not “this creature exists”. Filing a report under “apparitional experience” should mean “someone reported perceiving a figure or presence”, not “a dead person appeared”. Filing a report under “UAP” should mean “not immediately identified from available data”, not “alien craft”. These distinctions are not pedantry. They are what keep Fortean taxonomy from becoming either credulous mythology or reflexive dismissal.

## A Fortean Taxonomy That Keeps the Strangeness Intact

The best Fortean taxonomy is provisional, multi-tagged and evidence-aware. It should help a reader move through the field without pretending that every anomaly has a stable natural home. The category is a map, not the territory; the weirdness often lies in the mismatch between the map and the report.

A useful Fortean classification system should follow five principles.

**First, preserve the original claim before interpreting it.** Record what witnesses, newspapers, officials or later storytellers actually said before translating the case into modern categories. Fort’s own value as a collector lies partly in his preservation of awkward reports that might otherwise have disappeared into dismissal or ridicule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/22472" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

**Second, allow more than one category per case.** A phantom animal can be a cryptozoological claim, a local legend and a media panic. A UFO encounter can be an aerial observation, a psychological experience, a technological misidentification and a folklore narrative. A haunted road can be transport history, ghostlore, accident memory and landscape legend.

**Third, separate evidence from explanation.** A photograph, a trace mark, a witness statement and a local legend do different kinds of work. They should not be merged into a single credibility score too early. NASA’s UAP work shows why metadata, calibration and repeated observations matter; without them, even intriguing reports can remain analytically weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Independent Study Team Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Independent Study Team Report</span></span></span>

**Fourth, keep a category for the unresolved without glamorising it.** “Unresolved” should not mean “paranormal by default”. It should mean the current evidence does not support a firm identification. AARO’s public framing of UAP as an all-domain anomaly-resolution problem is useful here because it treats uncertainty as something to be investigated, not automatically celebrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

**Fifth, keep the cultural life of the case in view.** Some Fortean reports matter because of what happened; others matter because of how people kept telling them. Folklore, journalism, official silence, sceptical debunking, local pride and internet circulation can all become part of the phenomenon. A taxonomy that records only the alleged object or event misses the social afterlife that often makes a case Fortean in the first place.

The result is not a perfect cabinet of curiosities. It is a working index of uncertainty. Forteana needs categories because readers need orientation, archives need structure and investigators need comparable data. But it also needs porous edges, because the field’s most memorable cases are often the ones that expose the limits of the filing system itself.

    if (!cfg) return '#';
    var template = String(cfg.url_template || 'https://www.ebay.com/sch/i.html?_nkw={query}');
    var url = template.replace('{query}', encodeQuery(query || '')).replace('{query_plain}', String(query || ''));
    var joiner = url.indexOf('?') >= 0 ? '&' : '?';
    var params = [];
    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
