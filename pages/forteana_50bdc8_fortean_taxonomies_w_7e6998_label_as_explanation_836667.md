## Introduction

In Fortean research, labels such as *ghost*, *UFO*, *cryptid* and *miracle* are meant to organise reports, not explain them. The problem begins when a filing category quietly becomes a causal claim. A witness may report an unexplained light, a strange animal or an unusual [experience]({{ 'experience/' | relative_url }}), but once the case is labelled, readers often start treating the label as if it has already answered what happened. The mystery shifts from "What was observed?" to "What kind of ghost was it?" or "Which cryptid was seen?", even though the underlying event may still be poorly understood.

This is one of the most persistent traps in Fortean thinking. Good classification helps compare similar reports and identify patterns, but bad classification creates the illusion that uncertainty has disappeared. [Charles Fort]({{ 'charles-fort/' | relative_url }})'s own approach was far more cautious: he collected anomalies precisely because he believed premature explanations often concealed rather than resolved the interesting questions. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fort" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Charles Fort</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charles Fort</span></span></span>

## Why Category Names Feel Explanatory

Human beings naturally use categories to simplify unfamiliar experiences. Psychologists have long recognised that naming something reduces uncertainty, even when the name describes only appearance rather than cause. Within [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), this tendency is especially powerful because many reports begin with incomplete observations made under unusual conditions.

A witness may see:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an unidentified light moving across the sky;
* a large animal glimpsed briefly in poor visibility;
* an apparently impossible coincidence;
* an unexplained sound or apparition.

</div>

None of these observations inherently identifies a cause. Yet the moment someone writes "UFO sighting", "[Bigfoot]({{ 'bigfoot/' | relative_url }}) encounter" or "haunting", the report acquires a narrative framework. Readers begin filling in assumptions that were never part of the original evidence.

This shift is subtle because the vocabulary itself appears neutral. Strictly speaking, "unidentified flying object" merely describes an object that has not yet been identified. In public culture, however, "UFO" rapidly accumulated associations with extraterrestrial spacecraft. Even researchers such as J. Allen Hynek repeatedly stressed that the classification of reports should not be mistaken for an explanation of them. His reporting categories were intended to distinguish kinds of observations, not to establish what produced them. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.inlibra.com/10.5771/9781538131459.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: inlibra.com">[Inlibra+2Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">inlibra.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Researching the Paranormalof categories for labeling reports and UFO incidents. The same mechanism affects nearly every Fortean category.

## When the Label Quietly Solves the Mystery

The danger is not simply inaccurate language. It is that explanation starts creeping into the investigation before evidence justifies it.

Instead of asking:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What exactly was reported?
* Which details are independently confirmed?
* What competing explanations remain possible?

</div>

discussion often jumps immediately to questions that already assume the label is correct:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which ghost was responsible?
* Which species of cryptid was involved?
* Which alien civilisation visited?
* Which miracle occurred?

</div>

At that point, the category has stopped functioning as an archive label and started functioning as a hidden conclusion.

This process narrows inquiry in two ways.

First, contradictory evidence is more easily ignored because it no longer fits the accepted story.

Second, alternative explanations receive less attention because the report has effectively been "solved" linguistically before being solved evidentially.

For Fortean investigation, this is particularly risky because many cases remain genuinely ambiguous for decades.

## Ghosts, UFOs, Cryptids and Miracles: Four Different Versions of the Same Trap

Although these labels refer to very different traditions, they often produce the same reasoning error.

### Ghosts

A witness reports seeing an unidentified figure in a building.

Calling the report a *ghost sighting* already implies survival after death, even if the original testimony merely described an unexplained visual experience. Other possibilities—including misperception, environmental factors, psychological effects, deliberate deception or an unidentified ordinary person—may receive far less attention once the haunting narrative becomes established.

The report itself may never have claimed a ghost was present.

### UFOs

The history of UFO research provides perhaps the clearest example.

Originally, "unidentified" simply acknowledged that investigators lacked enough information to identify an aerial object. Over time, public culture increasingly treated "UFO" as shorthand for alien spacecraft. ### Cryptids

Reports of unusual animals illustrate another version of the problem.

Someone may report an unfamiliar creature, an unexpectedly large cat or an animal seen only briefly. Filing the case under *cryptid* can be useful for comparison with similar reports. It becomes misleading only if the classification is interpreted as evidence that an undiscovered species exists.

The report belongs in the archive because identification remains uncertain—not because the mystery has already been resolved in favour of an unknown animal.

### Miracles

Religious traditions often classify extraordinary recoveries, visions or unusual events as [miracles]({{ 'miracles/' | relative_url }}).

For historians and Fortean researchers alike, however, "miracle" identifies a theological interpretation rather than an independently demonstrated mechanism. The observed event may be genuine while its ultimate cause remains disputed. Separating the report from the interpretation allows different explanatory frameworks to remain visible instead of collapsing into one conclusion.

## Preserving Competing Explanations

One of the strengths of careful anomalistics is that it deliberately separates [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) from interpretation. Researchers associated with anomalistics have argued that reports should first be described accurately before stronger explanatory claims are introduced. The discipline's emphasis is on evaluating extraordinary claims without assuming either supernatural or conventional explanations in advance. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalistics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

A useful Fortean record therefore keeps several layers distinct:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Observation:** what witnesses actually reported.
* **Evidence:** photographs, traces, documents or independent testimony.
* **Classification:** which collection of similar reports it resembles.
* **Interpretation:** proposed explanations.
* **Conclusion:** what, if anything, the available evidence justifies.

</div>

Keeping these layers separate allows competing hypotheses to coexist until evidence favours one over the others.

For example, an unexplained aerial report might simultaneously be classified as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* an unidentified aerial observation;
* a multiple-witness event;
* a radar-supported case;
* an unresolved investigation.

</div>

None of those labels requires an extraterrestrial explanation. Likewise, an apparition report can remain a documented experience without committing the investigator to either survival after death or psychological misperception.

## A Better Habit for Fortean Thinking

Charles Fort repeatedly resisted final explanations because he believed certainty often arrived faster than understanding. His collections of anomalous reports deliberately crossed conventional boundaries rather than forcing every case into a settled worldview. The enduring value of that approach is methodological rather than supernatural: preserve the anomaly until the evidence genuinely narrows the possibilities. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fort" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Charles Fort</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charles Fort</span></span></span>

Seen this way, [taxonomies]({{ 'taxonomies/' | relative_url }}) are tools of organisation rather than verdicts. They help researchers compare reports, identify recurring patterns and communicate efficiently across a vast archive of strange claims. They fail only when readers mistake the drawer for the answer. A report filed under "ghost", "UFO", "cryptid" or "miracle" should still invite the central Fortean question: what, exactly, happened before anyone decided what to call it?
