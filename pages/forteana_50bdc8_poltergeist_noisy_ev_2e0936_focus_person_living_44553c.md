## Introduction

One of the most distinctive ideas in modern poltergeist research is that the reported disturbances often appear to follow a living person rather than a haunted building. Instead of asking whether a ghost inhabits a house, investigators noticed that some outbreaks seemed to cluster around a particular child, teenager or emotionally distressed adult. This [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) gave rise to the concept of the **focus person**—sometimes called the **agent**—and, within [parapsychology]({{ 'parapsychology/' | relative_url }}), to the **living-agent** explanation.

The focus-person model does not settle the question of whether unusual events are genuinely paranormal. Rather, it shifts attention from places to people. Depending on the investigator's assumptions, the same pattern can support very different interpretations: unconscious psychokinesis, deliberate trickery, heightened attention to ordinary events, or the effects of family conflict and psychological stress. This person-centred approach remains one of the central debates in the study of reported poltergeist phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

## Why investigators look for a focus person

A recurring feature in many classic reports is that unusual incidents are said to occur primarily when one individual is present or nearby. The reported activity may lessen when that person leaves the house, attends school, stays with relatives or is otherwise absent. Investigators therefore began asking not only *what happened*, but also *who was present* when it happened.

The focus person is not necessarily presented as the cause of the events in an ordinary sense. Even investigators who accepted paranormal possibilities often regarded the individual as the centre around which the disturbances occurred, rather than as someone consciously producing them. This distinction was important because many of the people identified as agents were themselves frightened by the events and appeared to gain little from them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

The idea also explained why many alleged hauntings seemed unusually mobile. Reports sometimes described disturbances following a person between rooms, homes or even temporary accommodation, rather than remaining tied to one supposedly haunted location. Whether interpreted as evidence of paranormal agency or not, this pattern encouraged researchers to treat the human participant as the primary object of investigation.

## Children, adolescents and distressed households

Many well-known cases involve children or adolescents, especially during periods of family tension, illness, bereavement or major life change. This has led researchers from very different traditions to notice similar demographic patterns while disagreeing sharply about what those patterns mean.

Parapsychologists have argued that adolescence may coincide with periods of heightened emotional conflict, making it more likely—if psychokinesis exists—for unconscious effects to appear. Psychoanalytic investigators instead viewed the same age group as especially vulnerable to expressing stress through symbolic behaviour or unconscious acting out. Sceptics point to another possibility: young people possess both the opportunity and motivation to play pranks that adults may initially overlook.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

Importantly, the association is statistical rather than absolute. Many reported focus persons have been adults, and not every case involves obvious psychological distress. Even supporters of the focus-person model acknowledge that no single personality profile consistently predicts who becomes the centre of an alleged outbreak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

## Recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis as an explanation

The best-known living-agent hypothesis within parapsychology is **recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis (RSPK)**, a term introduced by researchers J. Gaither Pratt and William G. Roll in the late 1950s. The name deliberately replaced "poltergeist" because it avoided assuming that spirits or [ghosts]({{ 'ghosts/' | relative_url }}) were involved.

Under the RSPK model, the reported physical disturbances are interpreted as unconscious psychokinetic effects generated by a living individual under emotional strain. The process is proposed to be involuntary rather than intentional. Instead of consciously moving objects with the mind, the focus person is thought to externalise psychological tension through an unknown mind–matter interaction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

Supporters argue that this framework fits several recurring observations:

* activity appears concentrated around one individual;
* incidents are often brief rather than lifelong;
* emotional stress frequently precedes reported outbreaks;
* witnesses sometimes describe disturbances occurring close to the focus person rather than throughout an entire property.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

The theory, however, faces a major scientific obstacle. Psychokinesis itself has not been established as a reproducible physical phenomenon under controlled experimental conditions. Consequently, mainstream science does not accept RSPK as an evidence-based explanation for reported poltergeist cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telekinesis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Ordinary alternatives to the living-agent model

The same concentration of events around one person can also be explained without invoking paranormal forces. This is why investigators increasingly examine ordinary mechanisms before considering extraordinary ones.

One possibility is **intentional mischief or deception**. If a child or teenager throws objects, creates noises or manipulates household items, other family members may witness only the apparent result rather than the act itself. Once expectations develop, unrelated accidents can also become incorporated into the growing narrative of a "poltergeist". Researchers such as Frank Podmore and later Joe Nickell argued that many famous outbreaks are better understood in this way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Podmore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frank Podmore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frank Podmore</span></span></span>

Another explanation involves **attention and interpretation**. In households already experiencing stress, ordinary noises, appliance failures or misplaced objects may be noticed more frequently and linked together into a meaningful pattern. Once neighbours, journalists or paranormal investigators become involved, increased observation can unintentionally reinforce this process through expectation and confirmation bias.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Psychological approaches offer a further perspective. Rather than assuming deliberate fraud, some researchers have suggested that emotional conflict, family dynamics and interpersonal tensions shape both behaviour and perception during alleged outbreaks. In this view, the focus person is psychologically central to the case without necessarily producing paranormal effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

## What famous cases reveal

Several frequently discussed cases illustrate why the focus-person concept remains controversial.

The 1958 [Seaford]({{ 'seaford/' | relative_url }}) case became influential because William Roll interpreted some events as fitting the RSPK model while acknowledging that certain incidents might have involved pranks. Later sceptical reanalysis argued that the evidence was better explained by the family's young son deliberately producing the disturbances, illustrating how the same observations can support competing interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_G._Roll" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">William G. Roll</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">William G. Roll</span></span></span>

The [Enfield]({{ 'enfield/' | relative_url }}) case likewise centred on adolescent sisters, particularly Janet Hodgson. Believers viewed her as the apparent focus of genuine anomalous events, while sceptics argued that the strongest evidence pointed towards schoolgirl trickery mixed with sincere witness error. The fact that activity repeatedly centred on one child therefore became evidence for both the living-agent hypothesis and its sceptical alternatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

These cases demonstrate that identifying a focus person does not determine which explanation is correct. It simply identifies a recurring pattern that demands closer investigation.

## Why the focus-person model still matters

The living-agent explanation marked an important shift in psychical research because it challenged the assumption that all [poltergeist reports]({{ 'poltergeists-edffb6/' | relative_url }}) concerned spirits haunting places. By treating the phenomenon as person-centred, investigators redirected attention towards [psychology]({{ 'psychology/' | relative_url }}), family dynamics and the possibility—however controversial—of unconscious human agency.

At the same time, the model illustrates a broader lesson about evaluating extraordinary claims. A focus person may be psychologically significant without possessing paranormal abilities. The same observed concentration of events can be interpreted as evidence for unconscious psychokinesis, deliberate deception, heightened suggestibility or complex family stress. Distinguishing among those possibilities requires careful observation, independent corroboration and, where possible, controlled documentation rather than relying on the apparent presence of a single central individual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>
