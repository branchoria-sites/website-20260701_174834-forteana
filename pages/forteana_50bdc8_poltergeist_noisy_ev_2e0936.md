## Introduction

Poltergeist cases are hard to prove because their “evidence” is usually noisy, sudden and domestic: knocks in walls, objects found displaced, furniture said to move, glass breaking, lights flickering, voices, water, stones, fires, and frightened witnesses trying to describe events that rarely happen on command. Within [Forteana]({{ 'forteana/' | relative_url }}), they are a stress test for evidence itself. A case can involve sincere witnesses, real disturbances and careful notes, yet still fall short of proving a paranormal mechanism because the crucial event happened off-camera, in poor conditions, or around people who may have motives to exaggerate, misperceive or play tricks.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/forteana_50bdc8_poltergeist_noisy_ev_2e0936-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Poltergeists" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
The better question is not simply “are poltergeists real?” It is: what would count as good evidence for a claim built from bangs, movements and witness clusters? [Poltergeist reports]({{ 'poltergeists-edffb6/' | relative_url }}) sit at the awkward boundary between household physics, fraud, [psychology]({{ 'psychology/' | relative_url }}), folklore and psychical research. That is exactly why they remain classic Fortean material: they are dramatic enough to grip a street, but unstable enough to resist clean proof. The Society for Psychical Research notes that such activity is often rare, short-lived, domestic, tense, anecdotal and difficult to observe under good conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/poltergeists-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

## What the claims usually include

Most poltergeist reports are not quiet apparitions. They are “noisy” cases: sounds and physical effects are the core claim. A useful working definition is a recurrent disturbance in which percussive sounds and object movements are reported, often in a house, often around a particular person or family, and often with no agreed ordinary cause. The German word is commonly glossed as a “noisy” or “rumbling” spirit, but modern investigators have often treated the label less as a proven spirit category than as a shorthand for a case family.

Across case collections, two features keep returning: knocks or raps, and movement of objects. A recent review of case-collection work notes that Alan Gauld and A. D. Cornell’s large 1979 study treated “percussive sounds” and “object movements” as defining features; a later 54-case German collection likewise found striking sounds in 70% of cases and object movement in 87%.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.anomalistik.de/images/pdf/zfa/zfa2022_1_076_huesmann-schriever.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anomalistik.de">[anomalistik.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anomalistik.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">zfa2022 1 076 huesmann schriever</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">zfa2022 1 076 huesmann schriever</span></span></span> Barrie Colvin’s report on the Andover case also notes that rapping sounds appeared in almost half of the poltergeist cases listed by Gauld and Cornell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/andover-case-responsive-rapping-poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Andover Case: A Responsive Rapping Poltergeist &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Andover Case: A Responsive Rapping Poltergeist &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span>

The physical catalogue is broad, but the most memorable claims tend to fall into a few recurring types:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Raps, knocks and bangs:** Sounds from walls, floors, furniture or ceilings, sometimes alleged to answer questions.
* **Small-object movement:** Crockery, bottles, toys, stones, books or household items said to fly, fall, shift or appear in odd places.
* **Furniture disturbance:** Chairs, beds, shelves or cupboards reported as moving, shaking, overturning or opening.
* **Environmental oddities:** Lights flickering, electrical failures, water appearing, doors opening, cold breezes or smells.
* **Centred outbreaks:** Activity said to cluster around a child, adolescent, employee, distressed person or particular household.

</div>

The Seaford case in Long Island, New York, shows the classic pattern. From 3 February to 10 March 1958, the Herrmann family reported opened bottles, loud noises, flying objects, an exploding sugar bowl and an overturned bookcase. It became a media event as well as a mystery, with police, journalists and investigators trying to make sense of disturbances that seemed physical but would not settle into a repeatable demonstration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.umbc.edu/specialcollections/garrett/hauntings/poltergeists/seaford-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.umbc.edu">[library.umbc.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.umbc.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Seaford Poltergeist – Garrett Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Seaford Poltergeist – Garrett Collection</span></span></span>

The Andover case shows the other classic form: apparently meaningful rapping. In 1974, Colvin investigated a Hampshire council house where the main claim was not flying furniture but coded raps that seemed to give meaningful responses. The report says attempts were made to exclude normal causes, including observing objects closely while raps appeared to transfer to them. That sort of case is especially tempting because it seems to add intention to noise: a bang becomes more impressive if it appears to answer a question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/andover-case-responsive-rapping-poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Andover Case: A Responsive Rapping Poltergeist &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Andover Case: A Responsive Rapping Poltergeist &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span>

## Why witness evidence becomes unstable

Poltergeist cases often begin with witnesses who are not trying to launch a theory. A family hears a bang, finds a bottle open, sees a picture fallen, or notices a child frightened by events in a bedroom. The problem is that the evidential value of these observations changes quickly once a “case” forms. Attention increases. Neighbours, clergy, police, journalists, ghost hunters and sceptics may arrive. Children may realise adults are watching. Journalists may reward drama. Investigators may form loyalties. Ordinary household accidents may be reinterpreted as part of the pattern.

This does not mean witnesses are lying. It means the social setting becomes part of the evidence. Domestic cases are especially difficult because homes are not laboratories. They contain loose objects, hidden spaces, pipes, pets, tired people, family tensions, night-time noises, visitors, arguments and private motives. The SPR’s overview is unusually blunt about these difficulties: cases may be over before researchers arrive, access is complicated by family life and unsocial hours, media involvement can interfere, and investigators must judge both first-hand [observation]({{ 'observation/' | relative_url }}) and the reliability of what they are told.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/poltergeists-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

The “focus person” problem adds another layer. Many parapsychological interpretations have treated poltergeist outbreaks as centred on a living person, sometimes an adolescent under stress, rather than a dead agent. William Roll’s term “recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis” described the idea that distress could be externalised as physical effects. The SPR overview notes that this “living agent” model became favoured by many professional parapsychologists, especially where reported phenomena correlated with a person showing anger or distress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/poltergeists-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

For a critic, however, the same pattern points in another direction. If the disturbances happen around a particular child, teenager or unhappy adult, that person may be the source not of unconscious psychokinesis but of deliberate trickery, attention-seeking, dissociation, mischief or accidental cueing. The evidence can point to “person-centred” without deciding the mechanism.

## Fraud, psychology and physics

Poltergeist evidence is vulnerable because many of its headline effects can be produced by ordinary means. A thrown object is impressive if nobody saw it leave a hand. A knock is impressive if nobody checked the pipework, floorboards, neighbours or prankster. A voice is impressive if it seems disembodied, less so if the witness cannot rule out ventriloquism, throat-speech or someone speaking from another room. A moving lamp is impressive until strings, vibration or a rigged fitting become possible.

Fraud does not need to explain every report to weaken a case. It only needs to show that some key events were staged, or that investigators failed to control the obvious routes by which staging could occur. In the Enfield case, centred on a north London council house between 1977 and 1979, the dispute has never been just “believers versus sceptics”. Even sympathetic investigators Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair accepted that some incidents were faked, while critics such as Anita Gregory and John Beloff found the children’s behaviour suspicious and were unconvinced by the stronger claims. Searchable archive records at Cambridge University show that the SPR’s Enfield material includes correspondence, tapes, press cuttings and exchanges involving Gregory and Grosse, reflecting the extent to which the case became an argument over evidence as much as an investigation of events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/635607" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk">[archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">archival objects</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anita Gregory in Parapsychology Review (1983) - One (1) copy of another response from both Maurice Grosse and Anita Gregory in the SPR Jo...</span></span></span>

Enfield remains useful because it displays the whole problem in miniature. There were multiple witnesses, recordings, photographs, investigators and press attention. There were also children, pranks, disputed “levitation” images, allegedly paranormal voices, and disagreements among psychical researchers themselves. A famous case can therefore be evidence-rich and still evidentially messy. More material does not automatically mean better proof; it can also mean more opportunities for contamination.

Psychology matters in a different way. Some researchers in anomalistic psychology argue that haunting and poltergeist experiences can develop when ambiguous stimuli are interpreted through fear, expectation and belief. Lange and Houran’s 1998 paper framed paranormal or poltergeist experiences as arising from the affective and cognitive interpretation of ambiguous stimuli, and later work on “haunted people” has continued to treat some cases as mixtures of anomalous experiences, recognition patterns and psychosocial factors rather than as straightforward spirit activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9788641/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.879163/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on frontiersin.org.</span></span></span>

That approach is not the same as saying witnesses are foolish. A person in a tense household who hears repeated unexplained bangs at night may reasonably become alarmed. Once a pattern is named, every creak, dropped object or electrical flicker may be noticed. Fear sharpens attention but can also narrow interpretation. In a family setting, one person’s alarm can become shared reality, especially when children, neighbours and journalists reinforce the story.

Physical explanations also deserve more attention than they often receive in popular retellings. Houses make noise. Plumbing hammers. Heating systems click. Roads, aircraft and nearby work can transmit vibration. Shelves fail. Bottles can leak or pop under pressure. Electrical systems can be faulty. The Rosenheim office case in Bavaria is a good example of how “physical” can be both impressive and contestable: reports included telephone anomalies, swinging lights, electrical disturbances and moving fixtures, but critics later argued that Bender’s paranormal interpretation had not adequately excluded natural or fraudulent explanations, including claims that threads and mechanical interference could account for some effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosenheim_poltergeist_claim" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rosenheim poltergeist claim</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rosenheim poltergeist claim</span></span></span>

The Fortean lesson is that an ordinary explanation need not be tidy to be stronger than a paranormal one. Several messy normal causes — prank, vibration, faulty wiring, rumour, expectation, coincidence — may combine into a case that feels like a single agency.

## Why “noisy evidence” is not the same as strong evidence

A poltergeist case often looks stronger than a ghost sighting because it claims physical effects. A moved chair seems more objective than a shadowy figure. A broken bottle can be photographed. A knock can be recorded. A police officer or journalist may witness something. Yet physical traces are only useful if the chain of cause is controlled.

A photograph of a fallen bookcase proves the bookcase fell, not that an unseen force pushed it. A recording of raps proves that a sound occurred, not that no person, pipe, animal, neighbour or building movement produced it. A witness who saw an object in motion may still have missed the start of the movement. A claim that an object was “too heavy” or “too far away” for trickery depends on exact measurements, sightlines and observation conditions.

The strongest poltergeist reports try to close these gaps. The Andover investigation is notable because it focused on raps that were recurrent, observed at close quarters and allegedly responsive. Colvin’s abstract says natural causes were actively considered, including transfer of the raps to objects under scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/andover-case-responsive-rapping-poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Andover Case: A Responsive Rapping Poltergeist &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Andover Case: A Responsive Rapping Poltergeist &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span> Dullin’s 2024 phenomenological review similarly highlights cases with multiple witnesses, repeated observations and detailed scoring of testimony, while also showing how difficult it is to compare cases drawn from very different periods, cultures and report qualities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/3263/2181" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

Even then, “responsive” evidence has a special weakness. Humans are excellent pattern-makers. If raps answer through a code, investigators must guard against leading questions, selective recording, chance hits, cueing, and the tendency to remember correct answers better than failed ones. A sequence that feels intelligent in the room may look less impressive when all questions, misses, pauses, ambiguous knocks and failed trials are logged.

This is why poltergeist cases are so often argued from the negative: “we could not see how it was done”, “the child was watched”, “the room was empty”, “the police were baffled”. Such statements matter, but they are not the same as positive demonstration. They describe investigative failure to find a normal cause, not direct proof of an abnormal one.

## What stronger evidence would require

A stronger modern poltergeist case would not need cinematic drama. It would need controls. The ideal evidence would capture an event from before it begins, through the physical effect, to its aftermath, with enough independent recording to rule out the obvious alternatives. That means the boring details matter more than the spectacular claims.

A credible investigation would need:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Continuous recording before the event**, not cameras switched on after the shouting starts.
2. **Multiple angles**, so the source of movement or sound is visible rather than inferred.
3. **Control of access**, including who entered rooms, touched objects, opened windows or moved furniture.
4. **Environmental logging**, such as vibration, temperature, humidity, electrical faults and plumbing activity where relevant.
5. **Complete event logs**, including failed predictions, non-events, ambiguous noises and ordinary explanations found later.
6. **Independent review**, preferably by people with expertise in building faults, stage magic, audio analysis, psychology and interviewing, not only paranormal investigators.
7. **Careful treatment of witnesses**, especially children or distressed residents, so investigation does not reward performance or worsen household pressure.

</div>

This standard may sound severe, but it matches the size of the claim. A poltergeist case asks readers to consider that objects move, sounds communicate, or invisible agencies act in ordinary homes. The evidence has to be strong enough to distinguish that from trickery, misinterpretation and building behaviour.

There is also an ethical reason to raise the standard. Poltergeist cases often involve children, bereavement, family conflict, housing insecurity or emotional distress. Labelling a household as haunted can attract media attention, frighten residents and lock a family into a role. A sceptical approach is not merely hostile; it can protect people from being turned into characters in someone else’s mystery.

## Why the cases still matter in Forteana

Poltergeist reports are not worthless because they are unproven. They are valuable because they show how strange claims are made, amplified, investigated and contested in real settings. They force a question that runs through Forteana: how should we handle reports that are too detailed to dismiss casually but too uncontrolled to accept as proof?

The best cases contain genuinely interesting tensions. Seaford shows how a suburban household disturbance can become national [folklore]({{ 'folklore/' | relative_url }}). Enfield shows how witness abundance, recordings and photographs can still leave a case divided. Rosenheim shows how technical-looking evidence can be undermined by doubts about controls and fraud. Andover shows why investigators remain interested when sounds appear structured and recurrent rather than random. None of these examples settles the matter. Together, they explain why the category persists.

Poltergeist claims live in the gap between event and interpretation. Something may have been heard; something may have moved; someone may have lied; someone may have misread an ordinary cause; several of those things may have happened at once. Forteana is at its best when it stays in that gap without pretending uncertainty is proof. Poltergeist cases are hard to prove not because they lack drama, but because drama is exactly what makes their evidence noisy.
